Football can buy AI for every step. It cannot buy the link. (Part 1)

The first part of our study of AI in football. It gives an overview of where AI is actually being bought across a club's value chain, who supplies it, and where demand runs ahead of supply. Parts 2 to 4 follow.

Written by Marcus Puhalj

CASE STUDY

For two decades, football clubs have been buying software to help them decide. Opta has logged match events since the 1990s. Catapult, founded in 2006, put GPS vests on players in training. Video and scouting platforms such as Wyscout and Hudl let a recruitment department watch thousands of players without leaving the building. On the business side, ticketing systems, customer databases and sponsorship reports did the same for the commercial team. Data now informs who a club signs, how it trains its players and how it sells to its supporters.

Most of that software was bought one department at a time, from different suppliers, for different purposes. The scouting database rarely talks to the finance model, and the ticketing system rarely talks to the club shop. Artificial intelligence is now arriving in both places: inside the tools clubs already use, and as a possible way to connect them. This study asks where in football the need for AI runs ahead of what suppliers offer, and this first part maps the market along a club's value chain.

01 The value chain, and two jobs for AI

A club does seven things in sequence, supported by an eighth. On the pitch side, it recruits players, develops them from the academy to the first team and keeps them fit, and prepares the team for each opponent. Then comes the match itself, filmed, tracked and refereed, usually under arrangements bought by the league. The match is the product, and the business side turns it into revenue: it becomes content, which builds fans and matchday income through tickets, the stadium, the app and the club's customer database, and the fan base is what the commercial team sells to sponsors and retail partners. The back office supports every step: finance and spending rules, contracts, people and operations.

AI can do two jobs along this chain. The first is inside a step: a tool that makes one activity better, such as a scouting model, an injury forecast, automated highlights or a fan assistant. The second is as a connector between steps, so that information created in one activity is used in another: a supporter's ticket history shaping a retail offer, or a new signing's wages feeding the club's financial forecast. The first job improves departments. The second joins them up.

02 Where clubs are today

Clubs have started to organise around AI. Reading named the first Head of AI in professional football in November 2025, alongside a one-year collaboration with the vision-AI company Score. Manchester City followed in March 2026, appointing Paul Power, formerly of SkillCorner. Arsenal advertised in July 2026 for an engineer to turn AI research into coaching software.

Most clubs, though, are early. In PwC's 2024 Global Sports Survey, only 6% of clubs were already implementing generative AI in their business and 60% wanted to but had no plan, a lower share implementing than leagues, international federations or media companies. The industry expects to move fast: in a February 2026 survey by SportsPro and Sportradar, 98% of sports organisations planned to increase their use of AI within a year. Asked about ticket pricing in October 2026, Leeds United's chairman said: "we don't really use AI."

Where clubs do use AI in the office, it mostly comes from general providers rather than football specialists. VfL Wolfsburg runs more than 50 custom tools on ChatGPT Enterprise across operations, communications, marketing and HR. Cambridge United drafted and executed player contracts with Genie AI, a general legal tool. Liverpool uses SAS to combine web, app and social data for fan marketing. Executives at three clubs in Spain, the United States and England told The Ledger the same in November 2026: adoption is low, what they use comes from the large general providers such as Anthropic, OpenAI and Microsoft's Copilot, and their biggest difficulty is making their systems work together.

03 Who supplies AI to each step

The Ledger built a database of 32 technology suppliers that sell AI features into football, with the activities each serves, its named football clients, its funding and the clubs that publicly use it. Most are not AI companies in a narrow sense. They sell data, video or software, and build AI into it. The database is not a census, and client numbers are the companies' own.

On the pitch side, supply is deep and built for sport. Recruitment has eight suppliers in the database and development and performance seven. Much of their commercial foundation lies in computer vision and predictive modelling rather than generative AI. SkillCorner extracts player and ball movements from broadcast video, so a recruitment department can compare players beyond conventional statistics. SciSports reports more than 200 clients, including Go Ahead Eagles, PEC Zwolle and FC Twente. Kitman Labs raised $52m in 2021 for a platform that brings athlete information together, while Catapult supplies physical monitoring.

Tactics and match analysis is more uneven. The same data serves recruitment and coaching, but identifying a player is not the same as preparing a team, and tracking data is not a tactical recommendation system. Below the professional game, AI cameras from Veo and Spiideo, which film and follow play without an operator, are the most common way small clubs meet match-analysis AI at all.

The match itself is bought centrally. Genius Sports supplies semi-automated offside technology to the Premier League, and its European Leagues agreement covers 18 member leagues. Leagues increasingly buy for clubs too. The Saudi Pro League's deal with Stats Perform gives every top-flight club AI scouting and performance tools. Every Premier League and EFL academy uses Kitman Labs, and the USL Championship in the United States adopted Kitman for every club in June 2026. For suppliers, a league contract reaches an entire competition at once, and a rival must then displace an installed system, not merely offer a better algorithm. The advantage lies in the distribution agreement as much as in the software.

The business side looks different. Sport-built AI is thin. Blinkfire, Relo Metrics and GumGum use computer vision to measure the value of a sponsor's exposure, which clubs use to price and renew sponsorship deals. Club DNA reports more than 24 clubs using its software for contracts, finance and spending-rule compliance, and Foundry Works advertises similar services. Most supply comes instead from enterprise software not built for sport: Salesforce, SAS, Microsoft and Amazon Web Services. Above both sides sit the general AI assistants, from OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft and Google, which can be used in any step and which clubs mention most often.

The disclosed money follows the pitch. Funding rounds and acquisitions for suppliers in the database since 2021 total about $750m, led by Teamworks' $235m round in 2025, Genius Sports' $200m purchase of Second Spectrum and Catapult's agreement to buy IMPECT for up to $90.6m. None of the disclosed money went to a business-side AI supplier serving clubs. These figures need care: each round funds a whole company, including hardware, video and data rights, mostly across many sports. They are not a measure of what football spends on AI, which no supplier discloses.

04 What clubs want from AI

Supply is only half the picture. Asked by PwC for the single most important use of generative AI, sports executives chose content creation (38%), performance (23%) and fan engagement (18%). Only 8% chose operational efficiency, and 8% sponsorship. Asked what held them back, they named process, changing how the organisation already works, far more often (47% of top-three mentions) than people and skills (27%) or the product itself (26%).

The pressure behind that demand is financial. From 2026-27 the Premier League limits squad spending to 85% of football revenue, against 70% under UEFA's rule, and in June 2026 UEFA fined four Premier League clubs for breaching its limit. Championship clubs spent 96% of their revenue on wages in 2024-25. For clubs in that position, AI that improves a signing or protects revenue has a clear value, and AI that tells finance what a signing will cost over its contract has an urgent one.

The study turns this into a demand level for each activity: low, medium or high. Each is assessed on three measures: the value at stake, urgency, and signals of buying such as hires, tenders and deals. What buyers can afford is counted once, on the supply side. Demand is high for five of the eight activities. Tactics is medium: strong at the largest clubs, weak at the smallest. Commercial is medium too: high for the largest clubs, lower below them. The match is low for clubs, for the opposite reason: leagues buy it for them. For content, fans and commercial, demand could be assessed only for the largest clubs and for leagues, so for now those readings describe the top of the game; how demand changes with club size is the subject of Part 2.

05 The first gap: inside the steps

Supply is assessed the same way, on three measures: how many suppliers sell AI into the activity, counted from the database; how proven their products are; and whether prices suit the buyers. Whether a league buys for every club is shown separately, in Figure 3, rather than added to supply, because it matters only to clubs that rely on their league. The two levels are then compared, only where both could be assessed. The study treats demand two full levels above supply, high need against low supply, as a pocket.

On the pitch side, supply runs ahead of demand. Recruitment, development and tactics are all well served, and the match is bought by leagues rather than clubs.

The one gap sits on the business side, in the club office. The back office is the only activity where demand is high and supply only medium. Content and fans and matchday are balanced, with both high, and commercial is well served. No activity reaches a pocket. The back office is also where the evidence disagrees: executives in PwC's survey ranked operational efficiency last, while the clubs The Ledger spoke to described the office as where they use AI most.

06 The second gap: between the steps

Scoring each activity on its own misses the second job AI can do. Twelve of the 32 suppliers say they link activities. But they link them within one side of the club. Teamworks, Kitman Labs and Hudl connect recruitment, performance and coaching. Salesforce and SAS connect ticketing, retail and digital channels. Genius Sports and Stats Perform connect data at league level, not inside a club.

Much of the linking on the pitch side has come through ownership. Catapult's agreement to buy IMPECT adds football intelligence to its performance business. Teamworks bought Zelus Analytics in 2024, bringing development, squad building and match preparation into one platform, and says 90% of Premier League clubs use its software. Hudl, which already owned Wyscout, bought StatsBomb the same year. A supplier that already holds a club's performance data can sell it recruitment and coaching tools through an existing relationship; an independent specialist must show that its analysis is worth adding another system.

Tottenham Hotspur shows what the business-side version looks like. In October 2026 the club said it had replaced more than 30 systems and connected more than 10 platforms, so that ticketing, retail, hospitality and its app feed one profile for each of its 4.6 million fan accounts. Its AI assistant resolves a fifth of fan conversations on its own. It took two years.

Across the two sides, almost nothing connects. Only two suppliers link squad cost to the club's finances: Club DNA, and Miagen, whose planning tool pulls ticketing, sponsorship and broadcast data into wage and transfer planning but whose AI features are not yet live. The Ledger found no public case of a club using either to join the pitch and the business. The three clubs it spoke to named that connection as their main difficulty, and PwC's survey points the same way: the main barrier is process, not product. The second gap is larger than the first.

07 Who will close the gaps

The map shows two gaps. Inside the steps, supply meets or runs ahead of demand almost everywhere, and the one shortfall is modest and sits in the club office: the back office. Between the steps, the shortfall is larger. Clubs can buy tools for almost every activity, but not the link between the pitch and the business.

Football's AI problem is less a lack of products than a lack of connection. Until squad, fan and financial data sit in one place, AI stays a set of separate tools, and a club like Tottenham, which spent two years connecting its systems, remains the exception.

Three kinds of supplier are positioned to close it. Sport-built platforms such as Teamworks, Kitman and Catapult already join up the pitch side, partly by acquisition. General providers such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft and Salesforce are already in the club office. Leagues, which already buy data and tools for every club in Saudi Arabia, the United States and England, could buy the connection too. Or a new entrant, built for the bridge itself, could reach clubs before any of them.

The next parts test where that answer differs. Part 2 asks which clubs, by size, need AI, which can afford it, and who buys for those that cannot. Part 3 asks how that changes between regions where clubs, leagues or the state do the buying. Part 4 brings the findings together into the opportunities they create.

Football can already buy AI for almost every activity a club performs. The question is who will sell the connection between them: the sport-built platforms reaching from the pitch into finance, the general providers reaching from the office into the squad, the leagues buying on behalf of every club, or a new entrant built for the gap. Whoever gets there first will set how most clubs use AI.
What to watch
  • A product that bridges both sides:whether Teamworks, Kitman or a general platform links squad, fan and finance data in one product, and names a club using it.
  • League-wide AI deals:whether more leagues follow the Saudi Pro League and the USL Championship and buy AI tools centrally for every club.
  • Funding for business-side AI:the first disclosed round for a football-specific fan, commercial or back-office AI supplier.
  • Heads of AI:whether the roles at Reading and Manchester City spread, and whether they report to the football side, the business side or both.
