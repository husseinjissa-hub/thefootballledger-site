What do the football universities teach?

Clubs are hiring for data, preparing for new financial rules and joining multi-club groups. We mapped 253 programmes from 149 providers that train people for football's business side, from university degrees to club schools and online courses. Data is now one of the best-taught subjects, but mostly to analysts. Of the 91 degrees built to train the people who will run clubs, only six make it a core subject, and almost none teach multi-club management or the new financial rules.

On 18 March 2026, Manchester City appointed Paul Power, formerly director of AI at the tracking-data firm SkillCorner, as the club's first head of AI. Four months earlier Reading, then in League One, had billed itself as the first professional club to create the role. The hiring data points the same way. Data and analytics roles made up 3.2 per cent of UK football club job postings in the year to June 2026, more than double their 1.5 per cent share in 2018, according to Indeed. Clubs are changing what they hire for. The question is whether the courses that train football's workforce are changing with them.

What clubs now need

Football's hiring has grown fast from a small base. UK club job postings rose from 247 per million UK postings in 2018 to 714 in 2025, Indeed found, though football is still only 0.07 per cent of the UK job market. Most of that volume is still commercial, matchday and venue work. The change is in the mix, and four forces are driving it.

  • Data and AI. Data roles' share of postings has doubled since 2018. Performance analyst, data scientist and data analyst are the most common titles.
  • Multi-club ownership. More than 345 clubs worldwide sat inside a multi-club structure at the end of 2025, against fewer than 60 a decade earlier, according to UEFA. Group-level roles in recruitment, finance and data follow.
  • A new rulebook. The Premier League's squad cost ratio, which fines clubs whose squad spending tops 85 per cent of revenue, starts in 2026-27. England's Independent Football Regulator will license the 116 clubs in the top five men's tiers, with licences required from 2027-28.
  • Agents and governance. A record 16,117 people applied for FIFA's June 2025 agent exam, and clubs paid agents $1.37bn in 2025, up more than 90 per cent.

Not all of this shows up on job boards. Senior finance, compliance and group roles are mostly filled through headhunters, so posting counts understate them. But the rules have fixed start dates, and the multi-club structures already exist. The demand is coming whether or not it is advertised. So who is training people for it?

Who is teaching

We found 253 programmes from 149 providers that train people for the business, operations or data side of football, from three-year degrees to £60 online courses. They come from five kinds of provider, and each region leans on a different one.

  • Governing bodies. UEFA's academy alone runs more than a dozen programmes, and its Certificate in Football Management has more than 3,800 graduates. The FIFA/CIES programme runs through 22 partner universities on several continents and has more than 7,500 graduates.
  • Universities. The largest group, 95 programmes, from Liverpool's Football Industries MBA to Georgetown, Columbia and NYU in the United States.
  • Private institutes. In the UK these are led by UCFB, a college of the University of East London based at Wembley and in Manchester, with 31 of the 37 UK private programmes once its online arm, the Global Institute of Sport, is included. In Spain the Johan Cruyff Institute, which has taught roughly 10,000 students, runs a Master in Football Business with FC Barcelona.
  • Club schools. Real Madrid's graduate school, run with Universidad Europea, and Barça's Innovation Hub account for 28 programmes between them. Manchester City runs its own Sports Business School and, from this autumn, an executive master with Georgetown University, announced in November 2025.
  • Online and vendor academies. Courses from data firms such as Hudl Statsbomb and Catapult, and online schools such as Sports Data Campus.

Club ties are rarer than the marketing suggests. Most named clubs on programme pages are guest speakers or graduate employers. Of 78 programmes with a working tie to a club or league, 36 run through Spanish football: Real Madrid, Barcelona or LaLiga. Knowing who teaches is half the map. The other half is what they teach.

What they teach

We read each programme's published module list and coded it against the eleven needs a club now hires for. Half of all programmes, 127 of 253, are built mainly for general sport management, and 91 for commercial, media and fan work. Both are the volume end of club hiring, and both are well served.

Data is no longer missing. Fifty-eight programmes are built mainly for data and AI, and 60 make it a core strand. In the UK, Liverpool launched an MSc in Sports Business and Analytics for September 2026, and Loughborough London and Birmingham both run sport analytics and AI masters. Real Madrid's Master in Sports Analytics teaches Python, machine learning and computer vision, and its dual degree with Stevens Institute of Technology adds a year of business analytics in New Jersey. LaLiga's business school runs a master in digital transformation and AI with Globant.

So data is taught. The question is who it is taught to.

Where the gap is

The gap is in the business degree. The degrees that train football's future executives barely teach data: of 91, only six make it a core subject, and only three teach football data at all. Two smaller gaps sit beside it: multi-club management and the new financial rules are each taught in a handful of programmes.

Gap 1: data in the business degree. Data looks well supplied: 60 programmes make it a core subject. But most of them train analysts. To see what future executives learn, we narrowed the 253 programmes to the degrees built for football's business side. Of the 253, 126 are full degrees. Of those, 91 are built for management, commercial, finance, law or operations rather than for analysis, scouting or coaching. These are the 91 football-business degrees. For each, we checked the published module list for a data module: one whose title or description is about data analytics, statistics, business intelligence or AI.

Of the 75 that publish a module list, 45 have no data module and 24 have a single module or elective, usually general rather than football-specific. Only six make data a core subject. Even the specialists skip it. UCFB, the UK's largest football-business school, runs 19 of the 91 degrees, and none has a football data module. Georgetown's new executive master with Manchester City, at $79,200, has five courses, and none is a data course. Only three of the 91 have a football data module at all, one module each, at Liverpool and Birkbeck.

The six that do make data a core subject teach sport analytics in general, not football, and most are new: Liverpool's MSc in Sports Business and Analytics (2026), Loughborough London's sport analytics MSc, Real Madrid's dual degree with Stevens, LaLiga's AI master with Globant (2023), Goethe Business School's Sports Management MBA, which starts in April 2027 with Eintracht Frankfurt and the DFL, and NYU's MS in Sports Business, whose analytics electives cover baseball, basketball and American football.

Data is not the only thin spot. Set every need a club hires for against the programmes built for it, and most are well served. Three sit in the corner where demand is strong and supply is thin.

Gap 2: multi-club management. More than 345 clubs now sit inside multi-club structures. No programme we found covers running one, beyond a single module in a Brazilian federation executive course.

Gap 3: the new financial rules. Fifty-eight programmes teach club finance, but almost all of it is accounting and economics. Only six of the 253 have a module on licensing or financial-sustainability rules, and none names the squad cost ratio, which starts in 2026-27.

Gaps 2 and 3 need a module, not a new degree, and schools may already be adding them; published module lists can lag. Gap 1 is different. It is not a missing topic but a missing skill, in the degrees that train the people who will run clubs.

The contrarian read

The usual reading is that football education is behind on data. The map shows the opposite. Data is one of the best-taught subjects in football education: 60 programmes make it a core strand.

The problem is who it is taught to. Most of those 60 programmes train analysts: sport-analytics degrees, short courses and scouting or match-analysis programmes. The 91 degrees that train future executives barely teach it: only six make data a core subject, and 45 have no data module at all.

That matters because executives now make the decisions data informs. A sporting director signs off on a transfer built on a valuation model, and a chief executive approves the budget for a data department. Neither can judge that work if they never learned it.

The new financial rules and multi-club groups are a smaller problem. A rulebook can be taught in a module, or learned from a club's lawyers and auditors, and only a few dozen people run a group of clubs. Knowing how to use data takes years.

Data is widely taught in football education, but mostly to analysts. The degrees that train the people who will run clubs have barely started to teach it.

As clubs use more data in their decisions, will universities follow, and make data a core subject in the degrees that train football's future executives? Or will clubs keep learning it on the job while the business degree stays as it is?

Methodology. Programmes were found through providers' sites, course directories and press releases in October 2026, and coded from published module lists. This is not a full census: some UK universities, SDA Bocconi and several club academies could not be checked, and 16 football-business degrees publish no module list. A programme counts as teaching data if its published module list includes a module on data, analytics, statistics, business intelligence or AI; data used inside other modules, such as finance, is not counted. What to watch
  • A data-core business degree:the first MBA or master's for future club executives that makes data a core strand, not an elective.
  • Liverpool and Goethe:how many students the two new business-and-data degrees attract, and where their graduates go.
  • Georgetown's first cohort:whether the Manchester City executive master adds a data course after its first intake.
  • Club job adverts:whether sporting director and chief executive postings start asking for data skills.
  • A rules or multi-club module:the first football-business degree to add a module on the squad cost ratio or on running a group of clubs.
  • Indeed's next breakdown:whether data roles keep rising past 3.2 per cent of UK club postings.
