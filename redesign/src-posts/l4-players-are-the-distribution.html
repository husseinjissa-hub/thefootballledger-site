Footballers became investors. The funds are selling their names.

A generation ago, the famous story about footballers and money was bankruptcy. Today players are putting their own money into clubs, leagues and funds inside the game they play. That shift is real. But in almost every deal an institution leads, and what it wants most from the player is not his cheque. It is his credibility and his audience.

On 16 September 2026, Thibaut Courtois told Forbes why club owners take his calls. Owners see a businessman, he said, and "maybe they trust me more than a usual footballer." By then NXTPLAY, the platform he co-founded with Gonzalo Vila, owned part of three clubs: Le Mans, now in Ligue 1; CD Extremadura, added in May; and KRC Genk, where it took a minority stake and a board seat in June. Courtois was 34 and still starting in goal for Real Madrid. His story holds both halves of this article. Footballers are now investing their own money in football, which the generation before them rarely did. And the reason owners take the call is something a cheque alone cannot buy.

From broke to buyers

The change starts with the money. The average wage in England's top flight was £75,000 a year in 1992-93. The PFA put it at £1.16m by 2009-10, and the Global Sports Salaries Survey at £3.2m by 2019-20. Current estimates cluster around £3m. After UK inflation, that is roughly a seventeenfold rise in real terms. The average Premier League player now earns the whole 1992 average salary in about nine days.

The money arrived faster than the machinery to manage it. Keith Gillespie lost more than £7m to gambling. David James went bankrupt in 2014 after career earnings of roughly £20m. Wes Brown, a two-time Champions League winner, filed in 2023. The era's signature investment was Ingenious, a film-financing scheme sold to about 1,400 investors, Wayne Rooney, David Beckham and Gary Lineker among them, with a minimum ticket of £100,000. HMRC challenged the tax relief and won. Players lost money on a product they did not understand, sold by people who did.

That fear built an industry. The ex-players' charity XPro's claim that three in five players face bankruptcy within five years of retiring has never been verified; the PFA's own estimate is 10 to 20 per cent. But the fear was real, and it produced the PFA's business school, Louis Saha's AxisStars and athlete courses at Harvard and UEFA. Our cap-table piece mapped what the sharpest players built with that education. This piece follows the money that now goes back into football itself.

The money comes home

The Ingenious generation put its money into things it did not know. This one is putting it into the thing it knows best. Clubs, competitions and football technology now take a growing share of what players invest, and most of these deals have happened since 2024.

  • Clubs. Kylian Mbappé took majority control of SM Caen in 2024, at an undisclosed price. On 1 October 2026, Lionel Messi agreed to buy CD Eldense, in Spain's second tier, pending regulatory approval. Juan Mata holds stakes in San Diego FC and Melbourne Victory. Thomas Müller and Mats Hummels have invested in Estrela da Amadora. Gareth Bale is building a club-buying platform with Juggernaut Capital. Zlatan Ibrahimović's 2019 stake in Hammarby was the precedent, and it drew a fan protest at the time.
  • Competitions. Baller League raised €23m in January 2025, with Mason Mount, Diogo Dalot and four other professionals investing alongside the athlete fund Apex. Kings League has raised more than $160m. Both formats turn attention directly into media inventory.
  • Technology. Harry Kane joined OxeFit's round of more than $45m, and Seat Unique at a valuation above £100m.

So the first half of the shift is real. Footballers are not just earning more; they are investing it, and much of it goes back into the game that paid them.

How the money is pooled

Most of this money does not move player by player. It moves through vehicles, and they come in two kinds: the ones players run themselves, and the pooled funds institutions run around them. The disclosed numbers show how different the two are.

The player-run vehicles are real, and mostly small. Blaise Matuidi's Origins brought in about 50 athletes, N'Golo Kanté, Olivier Giroud, Paulo Dybala and Kingsley Coman among them, to back early-stage start-ups. Kylian Mbappé invests through Coalition Capital, the vehicle behind his Caen purchase. Lionel Messi has Play Time, a sports-tech holding company run day to day by a venture investor. Mario Götze's Companion-M writes cheques of €25,000 to €50,000. Iker Casillas runs an accelerator, SportBoost, and Courtois has NXTPLAY. None of these has disclosed a fund size.

The pooled funds are where the scale is:

  • CHAMP. Launched in April 2026 by L Catterton, the consumer-focused private-equity firm, with Patricof Co. It is a $500m vehicle with more than 250 athletes as co-owners. Those athletes have committed more than $50m, about a tenth of the total. No footballers have been named.
  • Gamma Waves Partners. Launched the same month by Andrea Agnelli, the former Juventus president, with Giorgio Chiellini and Rocco Benetton as co-founders. It reported €55m of commitments towards a €100m target, for minority stakes in clubs, competitions, sports IP and sports technology.
  • Apex. A €51m sports-tech fund with more than 100 athlete investors, an average of roughly €0.5m each, with Red Bull's investment arm as a partner. In December 2025 it announced a €300m private-equity fund to buy 20 to 49 per cent of clubs, at €12.8m to €42m a deal. That is a target, not a confirmed close.
  • LEAD Locker Room Fund. Launched in October 2024 at an eight-figure size with more than 20 athlete shareholders, Harry Kane among them. Its own release promises them "no direct due diligence burden".
  • The Players Fund. A UK venture platform with hundreds of current and former athletes, Serge Gnabry, Chris Smalling and Ben Stokes among its backers, and a £40m fund launched in 2023.

The pattern is consistent. Where players run the vehicle, it is small: angel cheques, accelerator places, one club at a time. Where the money is large, an institution runs it, and the athletes supply a minority of the capital. Gamma Waves is the telling middle case: a former Juventus captain co-founded it, but it is fronted by a former club president and backed by a fashion dynasty. In CHAMP, athletes supply about a tenth of the money. So they are paying in, like any other investor. But a $500m fund does not need 250 athletes to raise a tenth of its capital. It brings them in for something else as well.

What the funds are buying

The people who run these vehicles say plainly what that something else is. Wale Ogunleye, who runs UBS's athlete practice after nine years in the NFL, says ownership groups recruit athletes for "their reputation and the things the athletes have built", the part that cannot simply be bought.

Look at who leads the deals. EQT Ventures led Baller League's round; the players came in behind it. L Catterton runs CHAMP; the 250 athletes co-own it. LEAD tells its athletes they will not need to do diligence. Even Courtois, whose platform is player-run, bought into Le Mans alongside OutField, a Brazilian sports fund, with Novak Djokovic, Felipe Massa and Kevin Magnussen on the same cap table. The exception has an institution in it too.

Football assets are where that credibility pays fastest. A player who backs a software company gives the founders a name for a press release. A player who buys into a club gives it a name to use in a transfer negotiation, a sponsorship pitch and a season-ticket campaign at once. That is why the money keeps coming home: the asset and the audience are the same.

The contrarian read

The consensus says two things. Players got smart, and athlete-backed funds will beat the market. The first is true; the four sections above show it. The second is premature. No athlete-backed fund, in football or anywhere else, has published its returns. The outperformance claims that circulate online trace back to marketing copy, not data.

What a famous name does add is now measurable. A 2024 study of more than 2,900 start-ups that pitched on Dragons' Den in four countries found that the better known the investor, the better the company did afterwards: it was more likely to survive, drew more web traffic and made more sales. The effect held even after allowing for the investor's track record. A famous backer brings attention, and attention helps a company sell. The same pull helps a fund raise money and get into deals, which is why CHAMP, Apex and LEAD put athletes' names at the front.

But selling is not the same as returning. A fund's return depends on things no name supplies: which companies it picks, the price it pays, how it helps them grow and when it sells. That is the work of an investment team. A famous name can make a fund bigger. It cannot tell you whether the fund will grow its investors' money year after year, and the Dragons' Den study does not claim to: it measured sales and survival at the companies, not returns to the investors. That is why the big funds in section 03 are run by institutions. The institution supplies the team that picks, prices and sells. The athlete supplies the name.

The players are investing. But they are not what drives the return. They are the distribution: the credibility and audience that help a fund raise money and sell its companies. Whether that money grows depends on the team behind the name.

That is not a scandal. It is a trade, and each side gets something. The athlete gets deals he could not reach alone: a stake in Genk, a place in Baller League's funding round. The institution gets the rest. It charges fees on the whole fund, the athletes' money included, and it makes every investment decision.

Whether that is a good trade for the athlete depends on how much his name is worth to the fund. No fund discloses that number. Until one does, players are joining on standard terms without knowing the value of what they bring.

Two questions remain open. Will funds backed by players actually perform better? The first published returns from CHAMP, Apex or LEAD will answer that. And will players move beyond distribution to making investment decisions themselves, as Courtois is attempting with NXTPLAY's planned fund, rather than putting their money into someone else's? Until both are answered, the footballer is an investor with his money and a distributor with his name.
What to watch

  • NXTPLAY's fund:whether Courtois's platform raises outside capital, and whether a player-led vehicle keeps control once it does.
  • Messi and Eldense:regulatory approval, and whether the price and stake are ever disclosed.
  • CHAMP's first football deal:whether a footballer joins its roster, and on what share of the capital.
  • Performance data:the first disclosed returns from any athlete-backed fund, the evidence the category still lacks.
