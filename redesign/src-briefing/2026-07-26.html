Story 01

Liverpool is worth $6bn — without being sold.

Source · Bloomberg / FT / This Is Anfield · July 2026

Fenway Sports Group confirmed this week that it is in talks to sell a minority stake in Liverpool. A group backed by the Mittal family and led by Amit Bhatia wants a significant slice — reported at up to 49% — and Jeff Bezos has been sounded out to join it. The figure being discussed values the club at more than $6 billion. FSG says it intends to keep control.

Here is why it matters. FSG bought Liverpool for £300 million in 2010. A $6 billion mark is an enormous paper gain — but nobody is buying the whole club. Selling a slice lets the owner bank part of that gain, bring in fresh money, and carry on running things. It also fixes a price the market can point to, without a full sale ever having to prove it.

This is now football's default move at the top of the game. Minority-stake deals have climbed from 22% of all ownership transactions in 2022 to 36% in 2025. Owners are selling pieces, not control — and each deal quietly resets what every rival club is said to be worth.

The question As clubs become more valuable but remain difficult to monetize, are minority stake sales becoming football's preferred ownership model?
Story 02

A borrowed empire ends up in court.

Source · Front Office Sports / Inside World Football · July 2026

Eagle Football is John Textor's multi-club group, built by buying into clubs including Lyon, Botafogo, RWD Molenbeek and, until recently, Crystal Palace, with much of the expansion funded by debt. John Textor's Eagle Football group kept coming apart in public this week. Textor filed a lawsuit in Florida seeking at least $400 million from Michele Kang, who took control of Lyon in June (87.78% for $30 million). He alleges she engineered a plan to push the group toward insolvency so she could buy Lyon cheaply. At the same time, Lyon's new owners said a review had found more than €700 million of "suspicious" transactions from the Textor era, and the club's listed parent warned that its survival now depends on finding a new majority shareholder.

Eagle Football was the clearest example of the debt-funded multi-club model: borrow against future value, buy several clubs across different countries, and refinance later. That model has now ended in insolvency, a courtroom fight and an audit scandal. When the borrowing sits at the top of the structure, trouble at the centre can pull every club down at once.

The Ledger has followed the Eagle break-up before, but this is the fullest picture yet of what a debt-built network looks like when it runs out of road. It matters because more than a third of Europe's biggest clubs now carry private-equity, venture or private-debt money.

The question Eagle shows a debt-heavy multi-club group can collapse as one. Do investors now treat leverage as the real risk in multi-club ownership — or is this written off as one badly-run group while the borrow-to-build model keeps spreading?
Story 03

The Premier League swaps its rulebook for a spend cap.

Source · Premier League / Ankura · July 2026

From the 2026/27 season the Premier League is replacing its old Profit and Sustainability Rules with a Squad Cost Ratio. Clubs will be limited to spending a set share of their football income — 85% for domestic clubs — on wages, transfers and agent fees. Spending above that line into a permitted buffer carries a financial penalty.

The change is about timing. The old rules looked back over three years of losses. The new one caps spending in real time against what a club actually earns. It ties how much a club can spend directly to how much it brings in — rewarding clubs that grow revenue and squeezing those that don't. It also arrives after Manchester City's legal wins exposed weaknesses in the previous system.

Over time this reshapes how clubs trade and what they are worth. Bigger-earning clubs get more room to spend; smaller ones are held tighter. The rulebook that governs the richest league in the world has been quietly rewritten.

The question A spend cap tied to revenue gives the clubs that already earn the most the most room to spend. Does it make the league more sustainable — or does it quietly lock in the gap between the big clubs and everyone else?
Story 04

England's regulator names its first club.

Source · Lancaster Guardian / Regulation Tomorrow · July 2026

England's Independent Football Regulator moved from writing rules to enforcing them this week. It opened a licensing trial that runs to October, and it is set to issue its first-ever public censure — against Morecambe, over a failure to hand over required ownership and directors information. The club's owners have until 28 July to respond.

Until now the new regulator was mostly a rulebook. A public censure is its first real test of whether naming a club actually changes how owners behave. Every club across the top five divisions will need a licence to operate before the 2027/28 season, so how this first case is handled sets the tone for the whole regime.

Morecambe is a small club, now outside the tiers the regulator covers. The bigger question is whether the same tools will bite when it is a Premier League owner in breach, rather than a lower-league one.

The question A public censure is the regulator's first show of force. Does naming a club prove the new watchdog has teeth — or only that its powers look strongest against the smallest clubs?
Story 05

Investors go after the leagues, not just the clubs.

Source · SportsPro / SportBusiness / PitchBook · July 2026

The money moving into football is increasingly aimed above the level of the club. Serie A has quietly sounded out private-equity firms — Apollo, Ares, CVC and Sixth Street — about buying up to 49% of the business that sells its overseas television rights. Barcelona finalised a deal handing Sixth Street a quarter of its LaLiga TV income for 25 years. And CVC has folded its sports holdings into a single platform valued near €7 billion.

Buying one club is hard to make money from and increasingly expensive. Buying a slice of a league's media rights, or a whole sports platform, is a different bet — on the future value of the broadcasting income itself. For the clubs and leagues on the other side, the trade is simple: cash now in exchange for years of future revenue.

This is football selling tomorrow's income to solve today's problems. It works while the value of TV rights keeps rising. It looks very different if that growth stalls.

The question Selling future rights income raises cash today and hands the upside to investors. Is this smart financing while media values climb — or are clubs and leagues mortgaging revenue they will one day wish they had kept?
Story 06

The World Cup ends as the most commercial ever.

Source · SportsPro / FIFA · July 2026

With the 2026 World Cup finished, the final numbers are in. FIFA took in around $15 billion in total revenue, up roughly 70% on the last tournament. Sponsorship reached $2.8 billion, and for the first time every one of the 16 top global sponsor slots was sold. US brands supplied more than half of that sponsor money.

The World Cup is the benchmark for the whole sport, and this one reset it. It also confirmed where football's commercial centre of gravity now sits — North America, where the tournament was largely held and where the sponsors increasingly come from. That backdrop is already pulling deals across the game, from shirt fronts to broadcast rights.

FIFA is now using these records as the argument for an even bigger tournament in 2030. The record haul is both the evidence and the temptation.

The question Does the commercial success of the 2026 World Cup offer a template for leagues and tournaments to unlock new revenue — or is FIFA uniquely positioned to achieve it?
