The Record · Issue 07 · 02 Aug 2026

Who sets the terms.

Six stories from a week when the money kept pushing into football — but its members, its ownership rules and its financial regulators kept deciding the terms. FIFA's plan to sell a slice of the World Cup collapsed, a Liverpool investor had to give up another club to get in, and Saudi Arabia bought out the dealmaker who built its Newcastle takeover.

Story 01

FIFA tries to sell the World Cup — and its own members stop it.

Source · The Football Ledger / Al Jazeera / CNBC · Jul–Aug 2026

FIFA spent last week trying to sell outside investors up to a fifth of a new company holding the World Cup's commercial rights — a business valued at around $20 billion, with JPMorgan running the sale and Josh Kushner's Thrive Capital lined up to lead. Early on Saturday, it dropped the plan. What stopped it was not the market but football's own members: UEFA's 55 associations voted to boycott every FIFA competition until the idea was abandoned, and the confederations covering North America and Asia objected too.

The money was never really the point. FIFA had just banked more than $15 billion from the 2026 World Cup, so a $4.2 billion raise was minor next to what it earns. What a company with outside shareholders buys is permanence — a fixed structure a future FIFA Congress cannot simply vote away — and that is what the members moved to block. It is the first time private capital has been turned away at the very top of the game. We set out what was actually being sold, and why FIFA wanted it locked in, in our full analysis: Selling a slice of the World Cup →

The question FIFA's members used their leverage to block the sale this time. But every federation has a price — so will they hold that line, or does the next offer simply get repriced high enough that a future Congress votes it through?
Story 02

To buy into Liverpool, first sell your other club.

Source · This Is Anfield / Al Jazeera · July 2026

Fenway Sports Group confirmed this week that it is in talks to sell up to 49% of Liverpool to a group led by Amit Bhatia and backed by the Mittal family, at a valuation around $6 billion. FSG keeps control. But the telling detail came next: within hours of the talks surfacing, Bhatia gave up his shareholding in Queens Park Rangers.

He had to. Bhatia's QPR holding was just under 20% and carried no control — yet football's multi-club ownership rules still would not let him keep it alongside a stake in Liverpool. Even a small, passive interest in one club can block an investor from buying into another. The valuation makes the headline; the rulebook decides who is even allowed to write the cheque.

This is the quiet story of football's ownership market. Money wants in at the top, and the deals keep coming as minority stakes rather than full sales. But regulators — not price — increasingly set who can buy, and force cap-table surgery before a deal can close.

The question Bhatia had to give up a passive, sub-20% stake in a Championship club just to invest in Liverpool. If the rules are this strict even on holdings that carry no real control, are they protecting the game's integrity — or turning away capital the league could otherwise attract?
Story 03

Amazon outspends DAZN — so DAZN buys the plumbing.

Source · Ampere / The Streamable / Sportico · 2026

For years DAZN was the biggest spender on streaming sports rights. This year Amazon's Prime Video passed it. Of an estimated $14.2 billion the streaming platforms will spend on sports rights in 2026, Amazon accounts for 27% and DAZN 22% — a gap widened by the absence of a Club World Cup, into which DAZN had sunk a $1 billion bet.

DAZN's response was telling. Rather than chase Amazon on price, it spent about $100 million buying ViewLift, a company that builds the technology behind streaming services. It is a bet on owning the distribution — the pipes football reaches fans through — instead of just the rights that flow down them.

These are two different theories of where the money is in football broadcasting. Amazon is betting on owning the content. DAZN is betting on owning the infrastructure. For the clubs and leagues selling rights, the identity — and the strategy — of the biggest buyers is starting to shift.

For the wider media trend behind this, our analysis of why football's Netflix never arrived is here →

The question One giant is buying the rights, the other is buying the pipes. Which bet on football's screen economy pays off — owning the content, or owning the distribution?
Story 04

Saudi Arabia buys out the broker who built Newcastle.

Source · Gulf News · 2026

Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund has taken its Newcastle United stake from 80% to 85%, buying out PCP Capital Partners — the firm run by Amanda Staveley, who assembled and fronted the 2021 takeover. Staveley, the dealmaker who made the acquisition happen, is now fully out; the Reuben family's investment arm holds the remaining 15%.

This is not PIF taking control — it has held a controlling 80% since day one. It is the sovereign fund tidying its cap table, removing the broker who put the deal together and edging closer to sole ownership. The people who help a state buy a club are increasingly bought out once the club is settled.

What makes it notable is the direction of travel. PIF has spent recent years talking up diversification and inviting outside capital into its assets — including reported interest in bringing minority investors into Newcastle itself. Buying more of a club it already controls runs the other way.

The question PIF has been diversifying and inviting outside capital in elsewhere — yet here it is buying more of a club it already controls. What explains the reversal, and what does fuller ownership let it do next with Newcastle?
Story 05

A regulator, not the market, decides Lyon's fate.

Source · Goal / MarketScreener · July 2026

Eagle Football's Lyon are close to being thrown out of European competition for 2026/27. After a €186 million loss and a debt-fuelled scramble, the club failed to meet the financial conditions UEFA had set for letting it play. A French financial body had already relegated and then reprieved Lyon earlier in the summer.

The point is who is calling the shots. Lyon's future is not being set by buyers, sellers or results on the pitch — it is being set by financial regulators deciding whether the club has met their terms. When the borrowing is heavy and the losses are real, the rule-makers become the ones who decide if you get to keep playing.

This is the flip side of all the money pouring into football. The same regulators tightening the game's finances now hold the power to switch off a club's most valuable asset — a place in Europe — over a spreadsheet.

The question Hard financial rules are meant to protect the game. But if they push a historic club like Lyon out of Europe, they also weaken the competition and the broadcast product everyone sells. Where should regulators draw the line between enforcing discipline and preserving the league's competitiveness?
Story 06

A sponsor, an owner — and the same fund behind both.

Source · The Manila Times / PR Newswire · July 2026

Al Nassr, the Saudi club effectively controlled by the Public Investment Fund, has a new lead shirt sponsor for 2026/27: HUMAIN, an artificial-intelligence company that is also owned by the Public Investment Fund. The same fund sits on both sides of the deal.

For Al Nassr, the deal lands as fresh commercial income — another line on the balance sheet and the income statement. But it also shows how the Saudi model still works: for now, the fund that owns the clubs is largely the one paying to sponsor them. Genuine outside money — foreign sponsors and commercial partners bidding on their own terms — has been slower to arrive.

That is the real challenge as the Kingdom pours capital into football and builds toward hosting the 2034 World Cup. Sovereign spending can fill a shirt today; the harder task is turning all this investment into demand from third parties who are not already inside the tent.

The question With so much investment now flowing into Saudi football, and PIF still largely acting as its own main sponsor, how does the Kingdom unlock real foreign investment and commercial demand for its clubs' sponsorship and commercial deals?
