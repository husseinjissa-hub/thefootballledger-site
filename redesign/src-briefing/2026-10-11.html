Story 01

ANTA has bought 29% of Puma for about €1.5bn. None of the money went to Puma.

Source · Retail Dive / Reuters via Stockopedia / KrASIA / Puma / Calcio e Finanza · 27 January – 7 October 2026

A kit supplier pays a club for the right to put its badge on the club’s shirts. What a supplier can afford to pay depends on its own finances and on the people who own it. Puma, the German sportswear company, supplies AC Milan, among other clubs. Last week’s Briefing reported that Milan is in talks to return to adidas, and that Puma’s deal with the club runs to 30 June 2028.

On 7 October ANTA Sports, a Chinese sportswear group listed in Hong Kong, completed the purchase of 29.06% of Puma from Artemis, the holding company of the Pinault family. The deal was agreed in January. The main terms:

  • Price: €35 a share in cash, about €1.51bn for the stake. That implies about €5.2bn for the whole of Puma.
  • Premium: more than 60% above the €21.63 at which Puma shares closed the day before the announcement. Reuters reported that the Pinault family had wanted at least €40.
  • Control: ANTA becomes Puma’s largest shareholder but says it has no plans to make a takeover offer. It says it will seek representation on Puma’s Supervisory Board. ANTA has pledged to respect Puma’s independence, brand identity and corporate culture, and its chairman, Ding Shizhong, said ANTA has “confidence in its management team” and supports the strategic transformation under way. These are the buyer’s statements.
  • Price protection: Reuters, citing sources and Hong Kong stock exchange filings, reported that ANTA agreed to pay Artemis more, under a formula, if anyone bids for more Puma shares or takes the company private within 15 months of the deal closing.

The distinction this story turns on is between buying a company’s shares and putting money into the company. ANTA bought existing shares from an existing shareholder. The €1.5bn went to Artemis. Puma received no new money from the sale. Reuters reported, citing sources, that Artemis wanted to redirect capital to other sectors and had taken on high debt while diversifying away from luxury. On those reports, the seller got a premium and a guarantee, and the company got a new shareholder.

Puma’s own finances explain why football spending is the thing to watch. In the quarter to June, reported on 31 July, Puma’s sales fell 9.4% on an organic basis to €1.69bn. It made an operating loss of €53.1m, against a loss of €109.1m a year earlier. It guides to a full-year operating loss of between €50m and €150m. A kit deal is mostly a fixed fee paid to the club whether or not Puma sells the shirts, so it is a cost Puma carries while it is losing money.

ANTA has not said anything about football in the statements reviewed for this Briefing. What it has said is that it wants to share its retail and operating experience with Puma. It describes the purchase as part of its multi-brand strategy. Puma’s existing club contracts were signed under Artemis. The first ones signed or renewed with ANTA on the Supervisory Board would be the first evidence of what ANTA wants football to do for Puma.

The question Will ANTA’s arrival change how much Puma spends on football? The purchase put no new money into Puma, and a 29% stake with a board seat gives ANTA influence but not control. Puma’s clubs are paid out of a company that is still losing money. So the question is whether ANTA uses its seat to keep football as a core brand-building budget, or to cut it towards what a loss-making company can afford.
Story 02

Everton is for sale, 22 months after the Friedkins bought it. No asking price has been published.

Source · Everton / The Friedkin Group statement via ITV / Sky Sports / City AM / Calcio e Finanza / Everton FC accounts as analysed by The Esk · 9–10 October 2026

The Friedkin Group (TFG), led by the American businessman Dan Friedkin, completed its takeover of Everton in December 2024. It bought the 94% held by the previous owner, Farhad Moshiri. TFG also owns the Italian club AS Roma. Under TFG, Everton moved from Goodison Park to the new Hill Dickinson Stadium for the 2025-26 season.

On 9 October TFG said it is “exploring new investment options for Everton Football Club, including the potential sale of a controlling interest”. It said it would only consider “parties who we strongly believe will be the right stewards to take the Club forward”. The statement gives no price and no reason. City AM reports that Moelis & Company is running the process, and that TFG’s 2024 purchase valued the club at £400m. Sky Sports’ Alan Myers said he believes TFG wants between £800m and £1bn. That is his belief, not a figure from TFG.

A buyer would take on the club’s finances. The last accounts on file cover the year to 30 June 2025, the final season at Goodison Park:

  • Revenue was a club record £196.7m, up from £186.9m.
  • The reported loss was £8.6m. That includes a £49.2m gain from selling Everton Women and the Goodison Park stadium company to TFG’s own holding company. Without it, the loss would have been about £57.8m.
  • Borrowings were £468.5m, of which £127.6m falls due within a year. £341.0m falls due after more than five years, including £350m of stadium financing repayable by June 2055.
  • £450.8m of shareholder loans from the previous owner were converted to equity after the takeover.

The accounts for 2025-26, the first season in the new stadium, have not been filed. Whether any reported price includes the borrowings is not stated. Everton has also been ordered to pay Burnley compensation over earlier financial breaches, according to ITV.

TFG’s statement gives no reason for selling. Myers links the sale to the cost of Roma’s new stadium, which he puts at £1bn, and said “it’s all too much for them”. That is his reading. A second possible pressure is a UEFA rule: two clubs under the same owner cannot play in the same European competition in a season. The Friedkins own Roma as well as Everton. Calcio e Finanza reported in April that they had not pursued the usual fix, a blind trust, which takes the owner out of decisions at one club. Neither TFG nor the club has linked the sale to the rule or to the cost of Roma’s stadium.

The word in the statement that matters is “controlling interest”. It is not the whole club. City AM reports that a sale could mean a minority or majority stake. A buyer therefore has to decide what it is paying for: a stake in a Premier League club, a finished stadium that holds about 53,000, or the right to take on £468.5m of borrowings.

The question At the price Myers believes TFG wants, who buys a Premier League club whose underlying loss was about £57.8m, and what are they paying for? A buyer gets a new stadium and a record revenue line, and also the borrowings and the cost of competing. So the question is whether the stadium and the Premier League place are worth £800m to £1bn to a new owner, or whether the debt and the cost of competing pull the price below what the Friedkins hope.
Story 03

RedBird added $4bn to the Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery merger. Its total there is now $6bn. It also owns AC Milan.

Source · TheWrap / Calcio e Finanza / Bloomberg / The Guardian via C21 Media · 6 October 2026

RedBird Capital Partners is a US investment firm led by Gerry Cardinale. In football it owns AC Milan, which it bought in 2022 for about €1.2bn, and the French club Toulouse. At the end of August the Briefing listed it among the shareholders of DAZN, the streaming service. Football is only one part of what RedBird owns.

On 6 October Paramount, run by David Ellison, completed its acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery in a deal reported at about $110bn. TheWrap, citing a person familiar with the matter, reports that RedBird put in a further $4bn, taking its total commitment to Ellison’s Skydance to $6bn. The earlier steps were:

  • 2019: $275m, when RedBird first backed Skydance.
  • Paramount deal: $2bn, to help Skydance buy National Amusements and combine with Paramount.
  • 6 October 2026: a further $4bn, for a total of $6bn.

TheWrap also reports that the merger is backed by $47bn of equity, led by the Ellison family and RedBird, who together hold 100% of the voting shares. Three Middle Eastern sovereign wealth funds and the bank LionTree also invested, without a say in governance. Cardinale called the deal “a defining moment for the industry” and said RedBird’s approach would apply to the combined company’s “iconic franchises, premium original programming, and live sports rights”. The reports do not say which RedBird funds provided the $4bn.

The sports rights are the link to football. The combined company now includes Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery’s TNT Sports. Last week’s Briefing reported that Paramount, through CBS, holds the US rights to the Champions League until 2030 in a reported $1.5bn deal. The Guardian reported last November that Paramount+ won most of the UK Champions League matches from 2027, outbidding TNT Sports. On those reports, the group holds Champions League rights in the US and the UK.

That puts RedBird on both sides of the competition’s money. Broadcasters pay for the Champions League rights, and that money funds what is distributed to the clubs that take part. Milan is one of those clubs. The money can therefore run in a loop: RedBird capital helps fund a company that pays for the rights, and part of what rights buyers pay can reach clubs such as Milan. The reports do not say how much, if any, would reach Milan, and Milan had no European football last season. This describes the owner’s position, not its conduct. Nothing in the reports reviewed suggests RedBird has acted on it.

The balance between the two assets is the live point. Milan reported a loss of about €24m for the year to June 2026 and net debt of €145.3m. RedBird’s $4bn this week went into a media company. The football club is one of several assets a single firm has to fund and manage.

The question When a sports investor’s biggest commitment is a media company that pays for football rights, is its club a priority or a side position? Milan sits on the receiving end of the money the group helps to pay, and it has just reported a loss. The firm’s capital and attention are finite. So the question is whether a media group with Champions League rights makes Milan more valuable to RedBird as part of a sports and media portfolio, or whether a $6bn media bet leaves the club to compete for what is left.
Story 04

Tottenham wants a share of its partners’ growth, not only a sponsorship fee.

Source · Inside World Football / Tottenham Hotspur · 14 September – 7 October 2026

A conventional sponsorship is simple. A company pays a club a fixed fee each year for the right to use its name, its stadium and its audience. The club gets the money whatever happens to the company. The company carries the risk and the club gets none of the upside.

In September Tottenham launched Hotspur Labs with The Players Fund (TPF). The programme is not new this week, and the coverage reviewed carries no new fact. It is included here because it is a different answer to the same question as a sponsorship: what a club’s audience is worth to a business. As reported by Inside World Football:

  • It targets venture-backed businesses between Series A and Series C. Those are funding rounds: Series A is typically a company’s first large investment round, and Series C comes when it is already growing.
  • Every applicant is first assessed by TPF Labs, a new TPF division. A shortlist then goes to the club.
  • Selected businesses get access to Tottenham’s audience, technology and commercial infrastructure.
  • Tottenham calls itself the first Premier League club to use the model.
  • No fund size, terms or company names have been disclosed.

Tottenham’s chief revenue officer, Ryan Norys, described Hotspur Labs as “the next step on our journey to identifying more innovative ways to refine and diversify our revenue streams”. The club wants to back young firms early and “share in some of that growth”. TPF Labs’ managing director, Andy Marston, said the aim is for participating businesses to become “the Club’s headline partners in a few years’ time”. The launch coverage does not say what form the club’s share takes. Where the arrangement replaces a fee with shares, the club is exchanging a certain payment for a possible one.

No investment has been announced. Until the first one is, Hotspur Labs is a stated method and not a result. What it is testing is whether the club’s audience is worth more as a stake in the businesses that use it than as a fee from them.

The question Would this model give Tottenham a financial advantage faster than traditional partnership deals? A fixed fee is certain and paid in the season it is signed, while a stake pays only if the partner grows. So the question is whether the stakes can pay back faster than the fees they replace, and, if they do, whether other clubs will follow.
Story 05

Crystal Palace’s owners value the club at £900m. A stake bought last year implied about half that.

Source · City AM / Inside World Football / The Esk · 5–7 October 2026

Crystal Palace’s largest shareholder is the American businessman Woody Johnson, who also owns the New York Jets. He bought a 43% stake from John Textor in July 2025, after UEFA raised concerns about Textor also owning a majority of Lyon. The club had been moved to the Conference League for the same reason. Josh Harris, David Blitzer and the chairman, Steve Parish, are the other named owners.

City AM reported on 5 October that Palace’s owners have valued the club at £900m and hired the Raine Group, an advisory firm, to gauge interest in an investment or a possible sale. Sources close to the club told City AM that investment, not a sale, is the priority. Palace declined to comment. The paper says the owners cite rising valuations of sports teams, the scarcity of London clubs in the Premier League and Palace’s Conference League run. Story 02 covers Everton’s sale. Palace is the second Premier League club in a week to say it is looking for outside investment.

The comparison that stands out is with last year’s price. Johnson’s 43% was reported at £190m. That implies about £442m for the whole club, so £900m is roughly double. The two figures are not the same measure. One is a minority stake that changed hands in a deal. The other is the owners’ own valuation, and it is not reported whether it includes the club’s debt.

What the money would be for is clearer. Selhurst Park has a capacity of about 25,000. Plans announced in 2017 to rebuild the main stand and raise capacity to 34,000 have stalled and costs have risen. Parish has admitted that the financing is not fully in place: “We’re looking at different ways of funding, but that will be in place.” Palace has also agreed to buy a 27-acre site in Beckenham to expand its training and academy. On Parish’s own account, an investor at £900m would be putting money into a club whose stadium plan is not fully funded.

Compare Everton in Story 02. It has a finished stadium that holds about 53,000 and £468.5m of borrowings. Palace has a plan for a bigger ground and no full funding. Both owners are testing whether the price of a Premier League place has kept rising. The £900m is an ask set by sellers. No bid has been reported.

The question Would an investor pay £900m for a club that still has to fund and expand its stadium, and do the reasons the owners give justify it? The owners point to rising values, the scarcity of London clubs in the Premier League and Palace’s Conference League run. Against that, Selhurst Park holds about 25,000 and the rebuild is not fully funded. So the question is whether those reasons are enough to support £900m, or whether the stadium bill means investors will pay less.
Story 06

Lazio’s single-ticket income fell 45% in a season of fan protest. Season-ticket sales point to a bigger fall this season.

Source · Calcio e Finanza / Corriere dello Sport / Wanted in Rome · 7 June – 7 October 2026

A club earns from its stadium in two ways. Season tickets are sold before the season and paid for up front, so they are the stable part. Single tickets are sold match by match, so they depend on who turns up. Lazio’s organised fan groups, the Curva Nord, blame the club’s president, Claudio Lotito, for what they see as a decline. In September the Briefing reported on the Rome prosecutors’ inquiry into an alleged campaign of pressure on Lotito to sell the club. In June the Curva Nord groups announced a boycott of the new season: no season tickets, no home games except the derby, no club merchandise, and a call to cancel the pay-TV services that show Lazio’s matches.

Calcio e Finanza, the Italian financial outlet, has now reported Lazio’s accounts for the year to 30 June 2026, which cover the first months of the protest:

  • Single-ticket income fell from €14.3m to €7.9m, a drop of €6.4m or 45%.
  • Season-ticket income held at €8.5m, against €8.6m a year earlier, with 29,918 sold.
  • Total match income fell 21%, from €23.3m to €18.4m.

Season tickets held because they had been sold before the protest. Calcio e Finanza reports that fans began deserting home games en masse in late January, and counts eight home games played in a largely empty Olimpico. Subscribers had paid, and stayed away. The fall in single tickets is the walk-up crowd, the part of the stadium income that fans can withhold in the middle of a season.

The 45% cannot all be credited to the protest. Lazio’s stadium income for 2024-25 included home matches in Europe, according to Corriere dello Sport, and Lazio had no European football in 2025-26. The accounts do not say how much of the fall each cause explains.

The cleaner evidence is the current season, 2026-27. Il Messaggero, cited by Calcio e Finanza, reports that Lazio sold about 4,000 season tickets for 2026-27, against 29,918. That is a fall of more than 85%, and the lowest of Lotito’s time as president. At last year’s average of about €284 a ticket, 4,000 season tickets are worth about €1.1m, against the €8.5m sold last year.

Set against the whole club, the line fans are withdrawing is small. Lazio’s total revenue was €173.7m, which includes €39.3m of gains from selling players. TV rights brought in €88.5m. On those figures match income is about a tenth of revenue. The money that pays the club’s bills comes from broadcasters and from player sales, and a boycott of the stadium does not touch either. Lazio reported a net loss of €10m.

The question How much leverage do fans really have over an owner? The protest has emptied the Olimpico, but stadium income is about a tenth of Lazio’s revenue, and the larger lines are TV rights and player sales. So the question is whether a boycott that costs a tenth of revenue is enough to change an owner’s decision, or whether Lazio’s fans have shown they can empty a stadium without moving the man who owns the club.
