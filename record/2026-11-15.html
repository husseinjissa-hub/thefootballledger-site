The Record · Issue 22 · 15 Nov 2026

Plenty of sellers. Who is buying?

Owners of at least eight of the Premier League’s 20 clubs want investment or are open to offers, The Independent reports, just as buyer interest slows. Manchester United’s annual report shows Adidas would halve its payments if the club went down. Real Madrid budgets for record revenue of $1.57bn and a profit of $16m. Arsène Wenger will not stand for FIFA president, while UEFA’s hunt for documents on FIFA’s abandoned sale goes on in a New York court. The Glazers are in a three-way race for the Saudi club Al-Ittihad. And Fox has closed its $22bn purchase of Roku as bidding for the US World Cup rights reaches its deadline.

Story 01

Owners of at least eight Premier League clubs want investment. Fewer buyers are calling.

Source · The Independent, via Off the Pitch and Football Insider · 11–12 November 2026; City AM; AP; Manager Magazin

Club prices have been rising. In August Liverpool’s owners, Fenway Sports Group, agreed to sell a minority stake, reported at between 30% and 40%, to a group led by Amit Bhatia, with Jeff Bezos among the investors, at a reported valuation of between about $6bn and $7.5bn. Fenway paid about $400m for the club in 2010. In October Bayern Munich sold just under 6.7% in new shares for a reported $280m, which implies about $4.2bn for the whole club, against about $1.5bn when Allianz bought in 2014.

Now supply is rising too. The Independent reported this week that the owners of at least eight of the Premier League’s 20 clubs are seeking investment or are open to offers, from minority stakes to full takeovers. Some have prepared prospectuses, the documents a seller gives buyers to describe the business. The paper says buyer interest is slowing, partly because of global uncertainty. The full list is behind a paywall. Everton and Crystal Palace are named, and the Briefing covered both on 11 October:

  • Everton: its owner, The Friedkin Group, is exploring a sale of a controlling interest. City AM reports that the adviser Moelis is running the process, and that the Friedkins’ 2024 purchase valued the club at about $530m.
  • Crystal Palace: its owners have valued the club at about $1.2bn and hired the Raine Group to test interest. Sources close to the club told City AM that investment, not a sale, is the priority.

A seller’s price is a starting position. With several clubs on the market at once, a buyer can compare them, wait and walk away, which shifts bargaining power towards it. A club with a new stadium or a London address may still draw a premium.

The distinction the story turns on is between the prices already paid and the prices still asked. Liverpool and Bayern were rare stakes in clubs that seldom sell. Eight Premier League clubs at once is not rare. The report counts supply, not buyers, and the Briefing of 18 October reported the regulator’s estimate that investors in English clubs have lost about 41% of their equity. The first deals will show whether recent prices carry over.

The question Will more clubs coming to market push prices down after the rise of the last few years? Liverpool and Bayern were priced as scarce assets, and now at least eight Premier League owners want money at the same time. So the question is whether sellers hold out for prices like those, or whether owners who need to sell accept a haircut to get a deal done.
Story 02

Manchester United’s contracts show what relegation would cost. Adidas’s payments would halve.

Source · Sports Mole and The Esk (citing United’s 2025/26 annual report) · 1–11 November 2026; Sports Illustrated · 11 November; Sky Sports · 13 November; talkSPORT

Large sponsorship contracts in football are not fixed sums. Most carry clauses that cut the payment, or let the sponsor leave, if the club drops out of the top division or misses European football. For the biggest clubs these clauses rarely matter, because they rarely go down.

United were 18th in the Premier League after ten matches, with one league win, when they sacked Michael Carrick. On 13 November they appointed Mauricio Pochettino until 2029. None of this means United will finish in the bottom three or lose the Adidas deal. Opta’s model gave them about a 22% chance of relegation on 11 November, and Sports Mole reports no suggestion that Adidas has acted.

What the clauses show is the exposure. United’s annual report for the year to June 2026, as reported by Sports Mole and the analyst site The Esk, sets out the Adidas terms:

  • A minimum guarantee of about $1.2bn over ten seasons to 2035, or about $120m a year.
  • A deduction of about $13m for each season without Champions League football. Missing it in 2025/26 has already triggered one.
  • A 50% cut in any year United are not in the Premier League.
  • A right for Adidas to end the deal, on one full season’s notice, after relegation.

Adidas is the clause that has been published. The front-of-shirt deal with Snapdragon, reported at about $80m a year to 2029, and the other main sponsors have relegation terms the club has not disclosed, The Esk says.

The pressure point is the debt. United had about $915m of financial debt at 30 June 2026. The Esk reports that in the June 2026 refinancing the minimum EBITDA, a measure of operating profit United must show each quarter, was raised to about $166m a year from about $86m. Its own estimate, not a club forecast, has a relegated United close to break-even on that measure in its first year.

United’s commercial business has held up through a long run of weaker results. The club has not won the league since 2013, yet commercial revenue was about $421m in 2025/26, Sports Illustrated reports. Stefan Borson, a former adviser to Manchester City, said on talkSPORT that the Glazers have quietly started a sale with an asking price of about $6.6bn, and believe results do little to the brand’s commercial value. The club has not commented.

The distinction the story turns on is between a brand and a contract. The brand has survived years without a title. The one published contract is written to pay less outside the top flight. A kit supplier does more than pay a fee: it makes, markets and sells the club’s shirts around the world.

The question If Adidas halved its payments, would United’s brand still be immune to results? It has held up through 13 years without a league title, but a smaller kit deal could also mean less spent marketing and selling United worldwide. So the question is whether the brand keeps carrying the commercial business even outside the Premier League, or whether lower sponsor investment slowly wears it down.
Story 03

Real Madrid budgets for record revenue of $1.57bn. The forecast profit is $16m.

Source · 2Playbook, via Grada3 and Fútbol Centroamérica · 6–12 November 2026; TUTTOmercatoWEB · 10 November

Real Madrid is owned by its members, and its board presents each year’s budget to an assembly of member delegates, this year on 21 November. The figures here are the budget as published by the Spanish site 2Playbook, not audited accounts, converted at about $1.12 to the euro.

The main lines:

  • Revenue of $1.57bn, a club record. Of that, $1.40bn is ordinary income, up 4.7%, and $173m is player sales, also a record.
  • Salaries and amortisation of $791m, another record. Amortisation is a transfer fee spread over the length of the player’s contract. The total is 57% of ordinary income.
  • Net profit of $16m, against $29m in 2025/26.
  • Net debt of $1.53bn, down 3%. Of that, $1.51bn relates to the Bernabéu.

The renovated Bernabéu drives the growth. Stadium income, which covers membership fees, season tickets, ticketing, the museum, VIP areas and events, is budgeted at $470m. It was $406m last season and $196m in 2018/19. The reports give no interest bill or repayment schedule for the debt behind it.

The fall in profit is smaller than it looks. Last season’s $29m was helped by a one-off gain of more than $26m, from releasing money set aside for a land dispute, Grada3 reports. The thinner point is what this year’s profit depends on. The $173m of player sales comes largely from the direct or indirect sale of academy players, among them Nico Paz to Como and Víctor Muñoz to Liverpool, TUTTOmercatoWEB reports. The forecast profit is less than a tenth of that figure.

The distinction the story turns on is between revenue and margin. The Bernabéu has more than doubled stadium income in seven seasons, but on these forecasts it has not made the club much more profitable, because spending on the squad has grown alongside it. A members’ club does not have to maximise profit. But the biggest lever of the past decade, the stadium, is now built.

The question What is the next lever Real Madrid can pull, now that the Bernabéu is built? Stadium income has more than doubled since 2018/19, but salaries and amortisation have kept pace and this year’s profit still counts on record player sales. So the question is whether the next gains come from new revenue, such as more events and media, or from lower costs, for example by using AI in how the club is run.
Story 04

Wenger will not stand against Infantino. The fight over FIFA’s abandoned sale moves to a New York court.

Source · ESPN · 12 November 2026; Inside World Football · 13 November; Press Association, via The Korea Herald · 15 November

FIFA’s president is elected by its Congress, where each of the 211 member associations has one vote. The next election is on 18 March 2027, and nominations close on Wednesday, 18 November. Money matters in FIFA elections, because FIFA funds the associations that vote.

In July Gianni Infantino announced FIFA Forward Enterprise, a company that would run FIFA’s tournaments, including the World Cup. As reported by ESPN and Inside World Football:

  • Investors, with Joshua Kushner’s Thrive Capital as anchor and JPMorgan advising FIFA, would have paid $4.2bn for a permanent stake of more than 20%.
  • Each member association was offered $20m to approve the plan.
  • UEFA, Concacaf and the Asian Football Confederation opposed it, and FIFA dropped it within days. The Briefing of 18 October reported FIFA saying it was withdrawn permanently, and proposing $25m per association from its reserves instead.

UEFA has not let the plan go. In August it filed in New York, Florida and Colorado for documents from Thrive and JPMorgan. ESPN and Reuters report the aim is a possible criminal complaint in Switzerland against Infantino and other officials, over alleged mismanagement. On 12 November Judge Jeannette Vargas in Manhattan refused to let two of FIFA’s US subsidiaries join the case, finding they lacked a “direct, substantial and legally protectable interest”. She has not yet ruled on whether the documents must be handed over. FIFA says the court has not addressed the merits and that no documents have to be produced.

Arsène Wenger, FIFA’s head of global football development, whom UEFA had tried to persuade to run, has told UEFA he will not stand, the Press Association reports. Kohzo Tashima of Japan, a member of FIFA’s Council, is set to challenge. Infantino claims the unanimous support of Africa’s confederation, and Asia’s is reported to have dropped its opposition.

The distinction the story turns on is between the vote and the evidence. The vote will be cast by associations that are being offered more money from FIFA’s reserves. The documents, if a court orders their release, would show how FIFA tried to sell part of its commercial business and on what terms. No date has been reported for a ruling on them.

The question If Infantino wins a fourth term in March, does the plan to sell a stake in FIFA’s commercial business come back? A sale would bring outside money in now, in exchange for a permanent share of the World Cup’s future income. So the question is whether football would benefit from that money reaching the associations, or whether selling part of the World Cup would leave less for the game in every cycle after.
Story 05

Manchester United’s owners are in the race for Al-Ittihad. Western club owners are bidding to buy into Saudi football.

Source · Inside World Football (citing Al-Riyadiyah and Okaz) · 10 November 2026; Semafor

The Briefing of 25 October reported that Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund (PIF), which took control of four clubs in 2023, was in talks to sell 70% of Al Nassr to a group led by RedBird, and asked whether other clubs would follow. A third club is now in a sale process.

Al-Ittihad, from Jeddah, is one of the biggest names in Saudi football. Saudi newspapers report three groups in a detailed financial and administrative review:

  • The Glazer family, controlling shareholders of Manchester United, with the Saudi businessman Wael Jamjoom.
  • Prince Saad bin Abdullah with Josh Harris, the US investor whose holdings include the Philadelphia 76ers, the Washington Commanders and a stake in Crystal Palace.
  • Sidra Capital and BlueFive Capital, reported to have made the strongest financial offer.

PIF has not named a preferred bidder or said how much of the club is for sale. Okaz reports that completion could wait until the end of 2027. The bidder list comes from the Saudi press, not from PIF. The one completed sale sets the benchmark: Kingdom Holding agreed in April to buy 70% of Al Hilal for $224m, and completed the deal in September, valuing the club’s equity at about $320m.

What is new is who is bidding. Saudi money has reached English football as a buyer, through PIF’s Newcastle. Here the flow runs the other way. Two of the three groups pair a Western sports owner with a Saudi partner, and both owners are tied to Premier League clubs in this week’s Briefing: the Glazers at United, Harris at Palace.

The Al Nassr process shows how PIF prepares a sale. Semafor reported that PIF was clearing about $213m of Al Nassr’s debt through sponsorship deals with companies in its own portfolio before selling. The reports on Al-Ittihad do not say what the club owes, or whether PIF will do the same.

The distinction is between what a buyer pays and what it gets. If the seller clears the debt first, the buyer pays for a stake in a club with a clean balance sheet, and part of the cost of cleaning it sits with companies the state owns.

The question Will PIF clear Al-Ittihad’s debt before selling, as it was reported to be doing at Al Nassr? Clearing the debt makes the club easier to sell, but the money comes from companies PIF owns. So the question is whether the buyers pay a fair price for a club with a clean balance sheet, or whether the state is quietly paying part of the price for them.
Story 06

Fox has closed its $22bn purchase of Roku. It now bids for the World Cup owning its own screens.

Source · Fox Business, TheWrap · 6 November 2026; Inside World Football · 23 October, 4 and 10 November

Roku makes the software and devices that sit between a viewer and a streaming service in more than 100 million households. It decides what appears on the home screen and sells advertising across it.

Fox announced the deal in June. It closed on 6 November, ahead of the 2027 timetable. As reported:

  • $22bn in total. Roku shareholders received $95.30 in cash and 0.9799 of a Fox share for each Roku share, and now own about 26% of Fox.
  • A new division, Fox Digital Platforms, combines Roku with Fox’s free streaming service Tubi, which has 110 million monthly users, and its subscription service Fox One.
  • The US Justice Department cleared the deal after an extended review. Fox committed to keep carrying content from other companies on Roku, and to protect their data, for at least three years.

The timing matters for football. Fox has shown the men’s World Cup in English in the US since 2018. Tubi streamed some matches live this summer too. The Briefing of 25 October reported that FIFA had opened bidding for the US rights to 2030, with 2034 as an option, and that bids were due on 19 November. Estimates put the two tournaments together at up to $4bn. Netflix has been named as a possible bidder. Fox’s chief executive, Lachlan Murdoch, said on 4 November that Fox would look at the rights “in a disciplined manner”, a word Inside World Football notes broadcasters often use when they expect to be outbid.

In principle a match on Fox can now also be promoted on Roku’s home screen and shown on Tubi, as some of this summer’s matches were. Two things limit what that is worth. The next two tournaments are in Spain, Portugal and Morocco, then Saudi Arabia, with less convenient kick-off times for US viewers. And for three years Fox has promised to treat rival content on Roku fairly.

The distinction the story turns on is between owning the content and owning the screen. Netflix owns its screen and buys content to fill it. Fox now owns both a channel and a platform, and the reports do not say how that will change what it bids.

The question Does owning Roku make football rights cheaper or dearer for Fox? Its own platform lets Fox spread the cost of a match over more viewers and advertisers, but estimates for the next US World Cup rights are about four times the last deal. So the question is whether Roku lowers what Fox pays to reach each viewer, or whether the higher price of the rights outweighs it.
