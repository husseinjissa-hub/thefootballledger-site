Story 01

FIFA proposes $25m for each of its 211 member associations. It also says its plan to bring in private investors is dead.

Source · Inside World Football / Reuters via The Nation / beIN Sports / FIFA · 14–16 October 2026

FIFA pays its member associations, the national football bodies, through a development scheme called FIFA Forward. Each association gets a set sum per four-year cycle. In April FIFA budgeted $10m each for 2027-30, up from $8m. Each association has one vote at Congress, which elects the president. Candidacies for the next election close next month. The vote is on 18 March 2027.

This week FIFA’s Council met in Zurich and reviewed a larger plan, the FIFA Surplus Solidarity Programme. Inside World Football reports that the programme adds $15m per association after pressure from confederation leaders. Reuters reports that the Council reviewed the proposal without approving it; it returns to the Council in December and goes to Congress in March. The main terms as reported:

  • Total: $6.35bn for 2027-30, which Reuters says is more than double the planned funding.
  • Per association: $25m. Across 211 associations that is about $5.3bn, leaving about $1.1bn for confederations, regional bodies and other recipients.
  • Source: FIFA’s reserves. FIFA forecasts an unaudited surplus of $5.2bn to $5.6bn for 2023-26 and says it will keep $1.7bn in hard reserves.

The $25m is $5m more than UEFA’s president, Aleksander Ceferin, and Concacaf’s president, Victor Montagliani, asked for. Their joint letter proposed $20m, according to Inside World Football.

The reserves are the other half of the story. In July FIFA’s president, Gianni Infantino, announced FIFA Forward Enterprise (FFE). NPR reported in July that FFE would have been a separate company handling the commercial rights to FIFA’s tournaments, raising up to $4.2bn by selling minority stakes to private investors, to fund more money for associations. At this week’s meeting Infantino apologised for how FFE was handled. FIFA says it is withdrawn permanently and will not return in any form.

The distinction here is between money raised from outside investors and money paid from FIFA’s own reserves. FFE was presented as the way to fund more for the associations. The $25m is being proposed without it. Inside World Football argues that this undercuts FFE’s stated reason. That is an argument, and FIFA has not accepted it. What the reports support is that FIFA now proposes to pay associations more from a World Cup surplus than it budgeted in April. The tournament is reported to have generated more than $15bn, but that is revenue, not the surplus.

Not everyone is satisfied. Debbie Hewitt, chair of the English FA and a FIFA vice-president, said FIFA had not answered the FA’s questions, including its requests for FFE-related documents. She said of Infantino: “The man has to answer those questions, and if he doesn’t answer those questions, no, he’s not fit to be FIFA president”.

The $25m still has to pass the Council in December and Congress in March, and the Congress that votes on it is the one that elects the president.

The question Who decides how FIFA’s World Cup surplus is spent, and does the money make the associations stronger or more dependent? The associations now stand to receive two and a half times the budget set in April, and the vote on the programme sits in the same Congress as the presidential election. So the question is whether $25m builds associations that can plan for the long term, or leaves 211 voters relying on a funding line whose size is reset by whoever wins in March.
Story 02

Investors in English clubs have lost an estimated 41%. Lenders were paid £455m in interest in 2024/25.

Source · Independent Football Regulator, State of the Game report (as published by The Esk) / Calcio e Finanza · 13–14 October 2026

The Independent Football Regulator is the body set up in England to supervise club finances. Its first State of the Game report, published this week, covers the top five divisions. Two kinds of money fund a club. Equity is money an owner puts in and can lose. Debt is money lent, which carries interest and has to be repaid. The report shows how each has fared.

On losses, 94% of clubs in the five divisions made a pre-tax loss in 2025/26, on a mix of actual and forecast data. The average Premier League loss rose from £20m in 2021/22 to £44m in 2025/26.

On investors, the report estimates that people who put money into top-five-division clubs lost 41% of their original investment between 2021/22 and 2025/26. Over the same period the FTSE 100, the index of Britain’s largest listed companies, would have returned more than 36%. The report calls these indicative estimates. Behind them are 78 ownership transactions between 2021 and 2025 worth £7.2bn.

The money that was not put in as equity was lent. The report’s debt figures for all English clubs are:

  • Total debt rose from about £2.8bn in 2020/21 to £4.1bn in 2024/25, and is forecast to reach £5.1bn in 2026/27.
  • Interest paid rose from about £97m in 2020/21 to £455m in 2024/25, while gross debt rose 46%.
  • The implied average cost of that debt rose from 3.4% to 11.0%.

Debt grew by less than half and interest more than quadrupled. The difference is the price of borrowing, which more than tripled.

A second source of cash is transfer factoring. When a club sells a player, the buyer usually pays in instalments. The seller can sell the right to those payments to a lender at a discount and take the cash now. The report says factoring rose from £55m in 2020/21 to £779m in 2024/25. It identifies 12 lenders, and the largest five handled 93.6% of the activity since 2020/21.

The distinction here is between who absorbs a loss and who is paid regardless of it. On the regulator’s estimate, owners’ equity took a 41% fall. Lenders were paid £455m in interest in a single year. Last week’s Briefing reported that Crystal Palace’s owners value their club at £900m and that Everton’s owners are exploring a sale of a controlling interest. The regulator’s numbers do not say what either club is worth. They do show what the last wave of buyers lost.

The next buyer will be buying into a system in which most clubs lose money, borrowing costs have more than tripled and a small group of lenders stands behind the cash. Whether that buyer prices in the 41% or looks past it is the open point.

The question Who is the next buyer of an English club, and what are they underwriting? Past buyers lost an estimated 41% while lenders collected rising interest. So the question is whether the regulator’s figures pull asking prices down towards what clubs earn, or whether easy borrowing keeps prices higher than the clubs’ own results would support.
Story 03

Lenders are advancing cash against player sales that have not happened. The lender cannot force the sale.

Source · Calcio e Finanza, citing the Financial Times / Inside World Football · 15 October 2026

Selling a player usually creates a receivable. The buying club pays the fee in instalments, and the seller can sell its claim to a lender for cash now. That is transfer factoring. The lender is repaid by a sale that has already happened, because the buyer owes the money.

The Financial Times reports, as summarised by Calcio e Finanza, that a newer product is being pushed to Premier League clubs. It is called squad-value financing. The main features as reported:

  • Size: a loan of roughly a quarter of the value of the club’s squad.
  • Security: in some cases the collateral is the proceeds of future player sales, including players the club may not intend to sell.
  • No forced sale: the lender cannot make the club sell. If the club does sell, the lender may have a priority claim on part of the proceeds.
  • Lenders: Tifosy and Fasanara are named as having helped develop the model in Europe. 23 Capital is said to have resumed this lending, and Burnley is cited as an English example.

The reporting gives no loan sizes or deal values.

The distinction the story turns on is between lending against a sale that has been agreed and lending against one that has not. Factoring is repaid by a buyer who owes the money. Squad-value financing is repaid by a sale the club has still to find a buyer for.

That moves the risk. If the sale happens, the lender is repaid from the proceeds. If it does not, the reporting does not say what follows. In principle the club could sell a different player, refinance or ask its owner for cash. That is an inference from the terms, not a reported case.

Porto show how much can rest on the sales line. Inside World Football reports that the Portuguese club’s profit from player trading fell to €55.9m from €100.4m, and that it swung to a €21.1m loss. Porto is not a Premier League club and is not reported to use this financing. It shows what happens when one year’s sales fall short.

Calcio e Finanza notes that relying on uncertain future proceeds could deepen clubs’ dependence on player trading, as the regulator warns about rising losses and debt in English clubs, the subject of the previous story. The loan is repaid only if the club sells, and the lender cannot make it. The cost of a sale that does not come sits with the club.

The question When the security is a sale that has not happened, who carries the loss if it never does? The club gets cash for a signing now. The lender gets a priority claim on proceeds if a sale comes, but cannot force one. So the question is whether this stays a way to bridge a transfer window, or becomes standing debt that pushes clubs into selling players they would rather keep.
Story 04

Leicester City is for sale at up to £225m. The prospectus values the buildings and land at £224m.

Source · Inside World Football / Football Insider / Sky News via The Business Desk · 13–17 October 2026

A football club can be valued on what it earns, on what it owns, or on the league place it holds. In most sales the three overlap. Leicester City’s sale pulls them apart. King Power, the Thai duty-free group owned by the Srivaddhanaprabha family, bought the club in 2010 for about £35m. Leicester won the Premier League in 2015-16. After back-to-back relegations it plays in League One, where it sits 17th.

Citi is running the sale, which the reports call Project Lineup. Inside World Football reports that offers were due on Friday 16 October and that the asking price is up to £225m. The package, as reported:

  • The men’s and women’s teams, the King Power Stadium and two training bases.
  • OH Leuven, the Belgian sister club.
  • Land around the stadium earmarked for development, including a hotel and entertainment complex.
  • Debt: close to £300m of shareholder debt has been converted to equity in recent years, according to Inside World Football.

Citi’s prospectus values the physical assets at £224m, according to Inside World Football, and puts no separate value on the football teams. Against an asking price of up to £225m, that leaves about £1m for the football on the two reported figures. Both numbers are reported, not published, and the asking price is a ceiling, not a bid.

The football side costs money to run. Citi forecasts revenue of about £97m for 2026, and relegation removed the broadcast income that supported the old cost base. Football Insider’s Stefan Borson estimates shareholders could need to put in £25m to £50m a year until a solution is found. That is his estimate, and he says the burn rate is unclear.

The Friday deadline has passed, though Inside World Football also reports it was extended earlier to bring in more bidders, and the outcome has not been reported in the sources reviewed. No buyer has been named in the reports reviewed. Football Insider reports one unnamed US group is interested and that the owners value the club at more than £200m. With the debt already converted to equity, the owners, on the reported structure, would be the ones paid.

The distinction is between buying a club and buying its estate. A buyer who wants the land and the stadium can price them from the prospectus. A buyer who wants a team has to price the cost of running it in League One, and what it would take to climb out. On the reported numbers the asking price covers the first and does not price the second.

The question What is a league place worth when the buildings account for almost the whole asking price? The prospectus values the property at almost exactly the sale price, and the team is a League One side that shareholders may have to keep funding. So the question is whether the natural buyer is a property investor who treats the football as the cost of holding the land, or a football owner who pays for a route back to the Premier League that the prospectus does not price.
Story 05

Arsenal plan to cut about 60 jobs. Last season’s accounts show a £1.4m loss, helped by £81.2m from player sales.

Source · Inside World Football / Calcio e Finanza · 15–17 October 2026

When a club sells a player, it books the difference between the sale price and the player’s value in its accounts as a profit, in the year he leaves. Arsenal’s 2024/25 accounts show how much that line can matter.

Inside World Football reports that on 14 October staff were told of proposals to cut about 60 jobs, around 7.5% of roughly 800 employees, to save £5m to £7m a year. A formal consultation is under way, so the number could change. The reported details:

  • Where: non-playing departments, including some scouting and analysis roles.
  • Review: carried out by outside consultants. It began before last season’s title win and looked at the size and structure of the operation, not the playing side.

The report has no on-record club statement. In September the Briefing reported that Arsenal had brought in consultants to review costs.

The latest filed accounts are for 2024/25. Revenue was a record £691m and wages were £346.8m. The pre-tax loss was £1.4m, down from £17.7m a year earlier, helped by £81.2m of profit from player sales. Arsenal have reported a loss in seven consecutive financial years.

Without the £81.2m, and holding everything else equal, the pre-tax result would have been a loss of about £82.6m. The saving is a target for future years, set against filed accounts for a past year, so the comparison shows scale only. The £5m to £7m is about 6% to 9% of the £81.2m.

The distinction the story turns on is between a cost a club controls and an income it does not. Jobs are the club’s choice. The price and timing of a player sale depend on a buyer. On these figures the saving is small beside the sales line.

The question What can cost discipline change at a club whose result turns on player sales? Arsenal’s planned saving is small beside the profit it books from selling players, and the club has reported a loss for seven years running. So the question is whether the savings are a first step towards a cost base that can break even without selling players, or a small adjustment that leaves the result in the hands of the transfer market.
Story 06

Napoli’s owner says any buyer should come forward. No price or sale process has been announced.

Source · Calcio e Finanza · 13–14 October 2026, with the 2024/25 accounts as reported on 20 November 2025

Club sales usually begin with an adviser, a prospectus and a deadline. Napoli’s has begun with a television interview. Aurelio De Laurentiis has owned the club since 2004. Speaking to the Italian channel Tg5, as reported by Calcio e Finanza on 13 October, he said that if someone is ready to pay for the efforts of a quarter of a century, they should come forward and he will assess it (the Ledger’s translation from the Italian). He called European success the only one he still lacks. This is an owner’s statement, not a sale process: the reports reviewed name no adviser, price, deadline or buyer.

Calcio e Finanza writes, in its own words, that the club’s recent accounts show a deficit of €50m. It does not say which year. Its reporting last November put the net loss for 2024/25 at €21.4m, in a season with no European football. Napoli covered that loss from a voluntary reserve of €216.6m. In its two earlier Champions League seasons the competition brought in about €73m a year, according to the same outlet.

The money that is missing is for buildings. De Laurentiis is working on a new stadium and a sports centre, which Calcio e Finanza says Champions League income would help fund. No cost has been reported. Repubblica reported that De Laurentiis met Q8 about land for the stadium; the meeting was exploratory and no agreement has been reached.

Saying you are open to offers is different from a sale. It tests the price without committing to it. What De Laurentiis says he wants is payment for what he calls a quarter of a century of effort. That describes what he has put in. A buyer will ask what the club will earn.

Napoli has a sizeable reserve and a stadium that still has to be paid for. That puts the question where the money for the building will come from.

The question Where will the money for Napoli’s stadium come from? The club covered its last reported loss from a reserve of €216.6m, and Champions League income has been worth about €73m a year, but no cost for the stadium has been reported. So the question is whether De Laurentiis can pay for it from Napoli’s own results and reserves, or whether the stadium needs the outside capital his statement invites.
