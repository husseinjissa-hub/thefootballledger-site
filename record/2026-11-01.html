Whose price is it?

Betting companies paid Brazil’s 20 largest clubs about $212m in 2025, 34% of their marketing revenue, and sponsorship deals with them became unlawful on 6 October. Sheikh Mansour put about $137m into City Football Group about three weeks after the guilty finding was reported. Harry Kane’s Ballon d’Or drew record audiences. Milan and Inter named their new stadium after Generali, in a deal reported at $725m over 20 years. McDonald’s is leaving the World Cup after 32 years. And Concacaf is taking its US sponsorship sales back from MLS’s marketing arm.

Story 01

Brazil made betting sponsorship unlawful within two weeks. Betting paid about a third of its top clubs’ marketing income in 2025.

Source �· Inside World Football (citing Sports Value via Poder360, and Reuters) �· 1 and 28 October 2026

Brazil’s president can issue a provisional measure, a decree with the force of law at once that Congress must later confirm or let lapse. On 25 September the government signed Provisional Measure 1.394/2026. It bans fixed-odds betting, meaning bets at odds set when the bet is placed, and forbids its operation and advertising. Sponsorship contracts with betting companies became unlawful from 6 October.

At least 16 clubs had joined legal challenges by 2 October, Reuters reported. They argue the industry was already regulated and the deals already authorised. No ruling has been reported.

Sports Value data, cited by Inside World Football, shows betting companies paid the 20 largest clubs about $212m in 2025. That was 34% of their combined marketing revenue. Dependence varied sharply:

  • Betting was 73% of São Paulo’s marketing revenue, 57% of Botafogo’s and 54% of Corinthians’. At Red Bull Bragantino it was 8%.
  • By value, Flamengo took about $50m, Corinthians $28m, São Paulo $21m and Palmeiras $19m.
  • Flamengo puts its own exposure at up to $80m and the cost to Brazilian football at about $460m. Those are the club’s claims.

Shirt space does not stay empty by default. 164 brands appeared on Série A shirts in 2025, up 24% from 132. Cimed, a pharmaceutical company, says it is in talks with ten clubs losing betting sponsors. That is the company’s own account.

The government says the ban tackles gambling addiction and household debt. The talks reported with clubs centre on restructuring their debt, which Inside World Football notes would not restore the lost income. These are reported discussions, not confirmed measures. Its reading is that Lula’s re-election makes a return to the old market unlikely.

The distinction the story turns on is between easing a club’s debt and replacing its income. A club that took 73% of its marketing money from betting must now find new sponsors or spend less. What clubs do next will show whether the ban slows Brazilian football or pushes it to build a broader commercial base.

The question What will Brazilian clubs do in response to the ban, and does it slow the development of Brazilian football? Betting provided 73% of São Paulo’s marketing revenue and 8% of Bragantino’s, other sponsors are reportedly in talks, and the state’s offer so far is about debt, not income. So the question is whether clubs replace the money with new sponsors and a broader commercial base, or cut spending on players and academies and lose ground.
Story 02

Sheikh Mansour put about $137m into City Football Group about three weeks after the guilty finding was reported. The price is the owner’s own.

Source �· Inside World Football (citing Companies House filings and City AM) �· 27 October 2026

City Football Group, or CFG, is the holding company that owns Manchester City and a network of other clubs. Sheikh Mansour controls it. Silver Lake, a US private equity firm, owns most of the rest. When a company issues new shares, the payment sets a price per share. If the buyer is the controlling owner, the price shows what he chose to pay, not what an outside investor would pay.

Companies House filings show 11,327,163 new CFG shares were allotted on 14 October, bought through Mansour’s Newton Investment and Development vehicle. His holding rises to about 81%. Inside World Football reports that the deal implies a valuation of about $8.1bn for CFG. That is the publication’s reading of the filing. CFG called the money “equity from our largest shareholder”, to be used for “normal corporate purposes against our strategic plans”.

About three weeks earlier, on 25 September, it was reported that an independent commission had found City guilty of 114 breaches of the Premier League’s financial rules. The Independent Football Regulator, or IFR, can force an owner to sell if a club has been run without the required propriety. Its chair, David Kogan, says it has “absolute power” to do so, but will wait for City’s appeal to finish.

CFG’s chairman, Khaldoon Al Mubarak, said earlier this year that the group would fetch at least $10bn on the open market, and that there was no wish to sell. That is a claim, not a price anyone has paid. The reports do not say whether he meant equity value, the worth of the shares, or enterprise value, which includes debt. So the two figures may not be comparable. They differ by about $1.9bn.

The distinction the story turns on is between a price set by an outside buyer and a price set by an owner adding money. No outside investor bought anything here. The controlling shareholder paid for new shares while a sanction is pending. That may show confidence in the club’s position, or it may be a way of putting cash into the group without selling to anyone. The reports do not say which.

The question How is the regulator likely to respond to the new money from Mansour? The IFR says it can force a sale if a club is run without the required propriety, and that it will wait for City’s appeal. The injection shows a controlling owner putting capital in, not stepping back. So the question is whether the regulator reads the money as a sign of a committed, well-funded owner, or as one more reason to look closely at who controls the group once the appeal ends.
Story 03

Harry Kane’s Ballon d’Or set audience records. The open question is what that audience is worth.

Source �· Sportbusiness and allfootballapp (Médiamétrie), Streams Charts, Al Jazeera �· 26 to 28 October 2026

The Ballon d’Or is the sport’s top individual award. Harry Kane won the men’s award on Monday 26 October at a ceremony in London. He is the first Englishman to win since Michael Owen in 2001. The ceremony is a television and streaming event, so the first measurable result is its audience.

In France, the L’Équipe channel reported:

  • An average of 2.2m viewers, a 17% share. The peak was 3.1m, when Kane was named.
  • A year earlier, the Dembélé ceremony in Paris averaged 1.6m viewers, an 8.6% share, with a peak of 2.5m.
  • The 2026 start was delayed. The scheduled 21:00 to 23:10 slot drew 1.95m, an 11.4% share.

Streaming measured by Streams Charts added more than 11.5m hours watched on Twitch, YouTube and Kick. The peak was 4.66m concurrent viewers, reached after DAZN’s broadcast had ended. Most of the audience, 88% of hours, was in watch parties run by other channels, more than 2,600 of them. DAZN Football’s own YouTube channel peaked above 1.8m, a record for the channel. There is no earlier-year comparison.

None of these numbers isolates Kane. The 2025 ceremony had a different winner, city and time slot. The 3.1m peak is a French figure at the moment of the announcement, not a global audience. No US, UK or German ceremony audience was found.

The distinction the story turns on is between a captured audience and an existing one. The peak came after DAZN’s own broadcast ended, in watch parties run by other channels. That may mean people gathered for one moment. It may mean a large football audience was already there and chose where to watch. The reports do not say which. Audience figures like these are what buyers and sellers argue over when they price football rights. That is an argument, not a finding.

The question What does this audience reflect, and what could it mean for how viewership and rights are priced? A record that peaked when the winner was named, and gathered mostly in watch parties, could be a one-night audience for a single moment. It could also be a sign of a wider football audience that broadcasters have not yet priced. So the question is whether the record is a captured audience that does not carry over to regular rights, or evidence that buyers will pay more to reach football’s audience.
Story 04

Milan and Inter have named their new stadium after Generali. A reported $725m over 20 years would be a new benchmark.

Source �· Inside World Football �· 30 October 2026; ESPN �· 30 October 2026; Calcio e Finanza �· 22 September and 10 November 2025; Football España

Naming rights let a company put its name on a stadium in return for a fee, usually paid every year for the length of the contract. For a club, that is income it can count on for decades.

On 30 October AC Milan and Inter unveiled the replacement for San Siro. The announced facts are:

  • A 71,500-seat stadium next to the existing ground, which the clubs bought from the City of Milan in November 2025. It will be called Generali Stadium.
  • Construction expected to start in 2028, subject to approvals. First match targeted for the 2031/32 season.
  • Neither club disclosed the financial terms. Generali also becomes official insurance partner of both clubs.

The number is not official. ESPN, citing sources, reports a naming deal worth $725m over 20 years. Divided evenly, that is about $36m a year. The two clubs share the stadium, so each would receive about $18m a year if the money is split equally. The split has not been reported. Reported comparisons:

  • Juventus signed a ten-year deal with Allianz in 2020 worth about $115m, an estimated $8m to $11m a year.
  • Barcelona’s Spotify deal is about $22m a season, according to Football España.
  • Arsenal’s Emirates stadium naming rights could be worth up to about $27m a year, an estimate by a former Manchester City financial adviser.
  • In November 2025 Calcio e Finanza reported that Milan and Inter wanted more than $22m a year, with estimates of $28m to $33m.

If the report is right, the total is the highest of these. Per club, about $18m is below Spotify’s at Barcelona and the Emirates estimate, because two clubs share one stadium. Terms differ between the deals and some figures are estimates, so the comparison is rough.

The clubs have not announced a cost. A document they gave the city in 2025 put the budget at about $1.44bn, to be paid with equity and bank debt. That was before the final design. On those numbers, $725m is about half. The naming money arrives over 20 years, and most of the building is paid for before it opens. Inside World Football notes that the clubs’ existing stadium arrangements have restricted their matchday and hospitality income. The reports do not say how much the new ground is expected to add.

The question Is this the new reference price for stadium naming rights, and how much financial opportunity does the new stadium create for Milan and Inter? The total of about $36m a year is the highest in the comparison, but about $18m per club is below Barcelona’s Spotify deal, and the clubs have not said what the stadium costs or what else it will earn. So the question is whether other clubs now point to Milan’s deal when they sell their own naming rights, and whether the new ground becomes a lasting income gain for two clubs or mostly pays for itself.
Story 05

McDonald’s is leaving the World Cup after 32 years. It says the exit is unrelated to FIFA’s headlines.

Source �· Adweek and Inside World Football �· 23 and 26 October 2026

A World Cup sponsor pays FIFA for the right to use the tournament in its marketing, usually with exclusivity in its category. UEFA sells the same way, one bank, one airline, one beer, as the Briefing of 27 September described. A sponsor that leaves frees its category for someone else.

Adweek reported, and Inside World Football repeated, that McDonald’s is ending its World Cup sponsorship after 32 years. It began with the 1994 tournament in the United States. Adweek ties the decision to an overhaul of McDonald’s marketing model and a move towards its own intellectual property. Inside World Football reports that McDonald’s says it is “unrelated to recent headlines about the association” and that it will keep investing in sport through other channels. The reports carry no response from FIFA.

McDonald’s ran its biggest World Cup campaign this year, in more than 110 countries. On the second-quarter earnings call, chief financial officer Ian Borden said it “provided a lift to the business and generated excellent system excitement” but “underperformed versus our expectations”. The reports do not say what McDonald’s paid FIFA, or how much of the decision rests on the campaign result.

The distinction the story turns on is between a four-year tournament window and an asset that runs all year. Inside World Football says brands are weighing expensive, short World Cup deals against year-round partnerships with leagues, clubs and athletes. That is the publication’s reading, not McDonald’s. FIFA is also selling the 2030 cycle. The Briefing of 25 October reported that bidding for its 2030 US broadcast rights is open, with bids due on 19 November.

The question Who actually gains from ending the deal, McDonald’s or FIFA? FIFA can probably sell the freed category to another company. McDonald’s gets its audience only in the few weeks of the tournament, and its biggest campaign this year fell short of its expectations. So the question is whether McDonald’s is the winner, saving a four-year fee for a few weeks of attention, or FIFA, which can re-sell the slot to a buyer that wants it more.
Story 06

Concacaf is taking its US sponsorship sales back from MLS’s marketing arm. The agreement ends with 2026.

Source �· Concacaf announcement �· 28 October 2026; SportBusiness; Inside World Football

A body that owns a competition can sell its own sponsorship, or hand the job to an agency that bundles several properties into one offer. Soccer United Marketing, or SUM, is the commercial arm of Major League Soccer and works as that kind of agent for others. It has sold for Concacaf, which runs the game across North and Central America and the Caribbean. SportBusiness reports that SUM’s partnership with the Mexican football federation runs through 2028. In 2021 US Soccer announced it would end its SUM agency and bring sales in-house.

On 28 October Concacaf announced that:

  • Its exclusive US commercial sales agreement with SUM ends at the close of 2026. Both sides agreed not to extend it.
  • It will expand its sponsorship portfolio and strengthen its in-house sales.
  • SUM will keep working with Concacaf on the 2027 Nations League Finals, on 25 to 28 March at SoFi Stadium in Los Angeles, and the 2027 Gold Cup.

Concacaf also held its first commercial Upfront this month, a presentation of its 2027 to 2030 inventory to brands. The word comes from television advertising, where networks sell airtime in advance. The announcement gives no financial figures.

The distinction the story turns on is between an owner selling its own inventory and an agent selling it as part of a bundle. An owner keeps the whole fee but carries the risk that nobody buys. An agent can offer a brand several properties at once. SUM’s fee and any guarantee have not been disclosed, so the reports cannot say who gained more. Two bodies that once sold through SUM have now moved sales in-house. Two is not a trend. It does show that SUM’s clients can choose to leave.

The question What does this mean for MLS’s marketing arm, and how will Concacaf lead its commercial activity from here? SUM keeps two Concacaf events in 2027, the Mexican federation to 2028 and MLS, while Concacaf now keeps the full fee on what it sells and carries the risk. So the question is whether SUM is left with a narrower book that weakens MLS’s commercial position, or stays central while Concacaf shows it can sell as well alone.
