What football is worth when someone has to pay.

Six stories from a week when football’s assets were put in front of buyers — and the answers came back wildly different. Liverpool’s owners sold a slice and wrote the buyer a priced option on the rest, days before a German regulator ruled that fan control is worth restricting investment for. A multi-club vehicle filed for bankruptcy owing $2.7bn. A streamer paid for a Manchester United audience without buying a single match. And Spain’s top women’s league could not find a bidder at any price.

Story 01

Liverpool’s owners sold a stake — and wrote the buyer an option on the rest.

Source · CNBC / Sky Sports · August 2026

The Liverpool minority sale is now signed. Under a definitive agreement announced on Friday, 1892 Holdings — led by Amit Bhatia and backed by Jeff Bezos’s K5 fund, the Mittal family trusts and EE Capital — takes a stake of more than 30% for a reported £1.65 billion. Bhatia becomes vice-chairman.

The number in the headline is not the important one. The agreement carries an option for the buyers to move to majority control inside twelve months, at a reported valuation of around $8 billion. Fenway Sports Group has not simply raised cash against part of Liverpool. It has sold a minority and, in the same document, set the price at which it will sell the rest.

That is the shift worth noticing. A “minority investment” used to mean an owner taking money off the table while keeping the club. Increasingly it means an owner agreeing today what the whole thing will cost tomorrow — and handing the timing to somebody else.

The question If a minority stake now arrives with a fixed path to control, is FSG selling a share of Liverpool — or selling the club in instalments?
Story 02

Germany’s limit on investors survived — now four clubs are the problem.

Source · Inside World Football · August 2026

Germany’s 50+1 rule has survived the most serious challenge it has faced. The rule itself is simple: a club’s own members must hold a majority of the votes in the company that runs its football team, so an outside investor can put money in but cannot buy control. It is the reason Germany has no equivalent of Chelsea’s or Newcastle’s owners.

That is exactly what put it at risk. Germany’s competition authority had been examining whether the rule amounts to an illegal restriction on competition, and it had the power to strike it down. On 12 August it closed the case and let 50+1 stand — accepting that the rule does restrict competition for investment, but allowing it anyway, on the grounds that keeping clubs answerable to their members is a good enough reason. That is an unusual thing for a competition regulator to put in writing: that limiting who can buy a football club is a feature, not a defect.

The clearance came with a catch, and the catch is the story. The rule only stays defensible if it is applied evenly — and today it is not. Leverkusen, Wolfsburg, RB Leipzig and Hannover 96 all play under exceptions granted for historical reasons. Those four were the rule’s showpieces. They are now its weak point.

The question Outside money has made rival leagues stronger over the past decade. Does keeping it out leave the Bundesliga steadily less competitive and less attractive to players and broadcasters — or is that a price German football has decided is worth paying?
Story 03

The vehicle that nearly bought Everton has filed for bankruptcy.

Source · Bloomberg · August 2026

777 Partners filed for Chapter 11 protection on 9 August in the Northern District of Texas, after creditors moved to force it into liquidation. Twenty-three affiliated cases were placed under joint administration the following day. The filing lists at least $2.7 billion of funded debt. Co-founder Josh Wander awaits trial in New York on conspiracy-to-defraud charges over an alleged scheme to pledge the same assets to more than one lender; he has not been convicted.

At its peak the firm held or had options over Genoa, Standard Liège, Hertha Berlin, Vasco da Gama, Red Star and Melbourne Victory — and came within a Premier League approval process of owning Everton. The clubs were not bought out of profits. They were bought with borrowed money, and when the borrowing stopped, clubs on four continents were left inside a vehicle that could no longer fund them.

This is the multi-club model’s first full-scale failure, and it is worth being precise about the lesson. The problem was never how many clubs one owner held. It was that nobody checked hard enough where the money to hold them was coming from.

The question 777 cleared enough checks to buy clubs in five countries. What would an ownership test have to look at — and who would have to look at it — to catch the next one before the clubs are already inside?
Story 04

Italy banned betting sponsors seven years ago. The bookmakers never left the shirts.

Source · Sportcal / Irish Times · August 2026

In 2019 Italy banned gambling advertising outright, in a law known as the Dignity Decree. Betting companies could no longer put their names on shirts, in stadiums or on television. It was the strictest ban of its kind in European football.

This month AC Monza’s new front-of-shirt sponsor is DAZN Bet Club. Fiorentina’s is Bet365 Scores. Venezia’s is PokerStarsNews. Juventus signed Bet365 Scores last month. None of this breaks the law, because none of these is a betting product — they are free scores services, news sites and entertainment brands, set up and owned by the bookmakers whose names they carry. The gambling company cannot advertise. A sister brand with almost the same name can. The logo on the shirt looks much the same either way.

England has just started its own version, and found a simpler route. From this season Premier League clubs cannot carry betting brands on the front of their shirts — but the ban stops there, and sleeves and training kit are untouched. So the brands have moved down the shirt rather than off it: Everton have kept Stake on the sleeve, and West Ham are shifting their betting partner the same way. No new brand names required.

The clubs still took the hit. Premier League sides face a collective shortfall of around £80 million in front-of-shirt income, deals for newly promoted clubs have fallen by roughly half since gambling money left that market, and several went deep into the summer with the space unsold. The ban cost the clubs real money. It did not cost the bookmakers their place on the shirt.

The question Italy has shown that a betting ban can be worked around with a change of name. Do clubs in other leagues copy it to win the category back — or does the money drift to the markets where the workaround already works?
Story 05

Disney+ bought a Manchester United audience — without buying a single match.

Source · Inside World Football · August 2026

Disney+ has commissioned a Manchester United show fronted by Wayne Rooney and Mark Goldbridge. It comes from The Overlap, Gary Neville’s media company, which earlier bought The United Stand — a fan channel with 2.27 million subscribers, built on YouTube by supporters rather than by the club.

What the streamer is paying for is not football. It is an audience that already exists, already turns up every week, and is attached to one club. Disney+ does not hold a single Manchester United match right and does not need one to be in front of United fans.

It is not a one-off. In the same week, KSI’s YouTube channel — 18.9 million subscribers — began carrying selected National League South matches, with DAZN distributing them in parallel. KSI is a shareholder in Dagenham & Redbridge, so this is an owner routing his own audience to a rightsholder rather than selling a right. Sixth-tier football, same logic as the Disney+ deal.

That is a meaningful crack in how football media has always worked. Match rights are the most expensive inventory in the game and the auctions have few winners; a fan channel costs a fraction and comes with the one thing rights cannot guarantee — a habit. It will not replace live football. But it is a way to build a fan base fifty-two weeks a year at a price a rights auction will never offer.

The question If a streamer can reach a club’s supporters without owning a match, are podcasts and fan channels becoming the real way to build a football audience — or just somewhere to wait until the rights come up again?
Story 06

Spain’s top women’s league could not sell its TV rights at any price.

Source · Sportcal · August 2026

Liga F put its domestic broadcast rights out to tender and neither lot attracted a qualifying bid. The reserve was €5 million for eight pay-TV matches a gameweek and €2 million for four free-to-air matches. Both went unmet. A second round opened on 12 August with a 19 August deadline, which leaves Spain’s top women’s league weeks from kick-off without a domestic broadcaster.

The context is not encouraging. DAZN walked away from its 2022-27 deal a year early, and the league distributed just €4.8 million in broadcast fees across 2024-25. These are not big numbers being haggled over. They are small numbers that nobody would meet.

Set that against England. The Women’s Super League is expanding from 12 clubs to 14 for 2026-27 and is reported to be considering borrowing tens of millions to fund that growth, while CBS has taken the US rights through 2029-30. Two markets, two verdicts on the same asset: lenders will advance money against women’s football’s future, and broadcasters will not pay much for its present.

The question If capital will lend against women’s football but broadcasters will not buy it, which of those two numbers is telling the truth about what the game is worth?
