Changing hands.

Six stories from a week when football spent less time on the scoreboard than on the plumbing behind it — who sells its broadcast rights, who finances its stadiums, and where the World Cup's record billions actually landed as the tournament closed.

Sundays
Story 01

The Bundesliga sells America reach, not fees.

Source · Sports Video Group / Awful Announcing · July 2026

Germany's Bundesliga signed a new five-year deal for its US television rights with USA Sports, part of the NBCUniversal spin-off Versant. A minimum of 30 matches a season will air on the USA Network cable channel; the other 250-plus games each year will stream for free on Fandango. The deal is worth about $20 million a year, or roughly $100 million over five years.

Here is the striking part: that is less money than before. The league's old ESPN deal paid around $30 million a year. In other words, the Bundesliga took a pay cut to put most of its matches in front of American viewers for free, pushing its reach past 80 million US homes. It is a clear bet that in a market where it trails the Premier League badly, the scarce thing is an audience — not cash. Build the audience now, and charge for it later.

That is the opposite of what a league normally does with its most valuable asset. It only makes sense if you believe attention today is worth more than money today.

The question The Bundesliga bet that free reach now beats a bigger cheque. Does giving its games away build a US audience it can eventually charge for — or does it simply train American fans to expect football for nothing?
Story 02

Serie A shops its media arm to private equity.

Source · SportsPro / Private Equity Insights · July 2026

Italy's Serie A, advised by the bank JP Morgan, is sounding out big private-equity firms — Apollo, CVC, Ares and Sixth Street — about buying up to 49 percent of the business that sells its overseas television rights. That unit brings in around €250 million a year, and the league wants to roughly triple that by 2030. A report to Serie A's 20 clubs is expected later this year.

Private equity means investment funds that buy stakes in companies. Serie A cannot grow those international fees fast enough on its own, so rather than sell the rights themselves, it is trying to sell a slice of the company that owns them — turning a stuck revenue line into upfront cash and outside expertise. Set this next to the Bundesliga story and a pattern appears: where the Germans are changing how they sell rights, the Italians are selling a piece of the machine that sells them. Both are admissions that the gap to the Premier League is not closing on the pitch, so it is being closed with financial engineering.

The question Serie A wants outside money to fix what it cannot grow alone. Does private-equity capital and discipline actually triple its international rights — or does selling a slice of tomorrow's income just paper over a league stuck in second place?
Story 03

The middlemen who sell football's rights change.

Source · Inside World Football / SportBusiness · July 2026

UEFA, European football's governing body, is close to replacing the agency that has sold the commercial rights to its national-team competitions — the Euros, the Nations League and qualifiers — since 2012. That agency, CAA Eleven, has been dropped from the contest for the next cycle, which runs to 2036. The two firms left standing are Relevent Sports and Sela — a company owned by Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund, PIF. UEFA is expected to decide in September.

These are among the most valuable assets in the whole sport, and whoever sells them sits at a chokepoint — the narrow point that every pound of rights money has to pass through, taking a cut and setting the price. Sela reaching the final two matters because it shows Saudi money climbing the ladder: from owning a club in Newcastle, to sponsoring the World Cup, to potentially controlling the layer that prices football itself.

Ownership of clubs makes headlines. Ownership of the sales layer is quieter — and arguably more powerful.

The question Football's rights-selling layer is quietly changing hands. If a PIF-owned agency wins UEFA's business, does Saudi capital gain a lasting grip on how the game is priced — or is this just one contract among many?
Story 04

The World Cup broke every record — and quietly got cheaper.

Source · SVG Europe / SportsPro · July 2026

The 2026 World Cup — the first with 48 teams, its final played today, July 19 — closes as the richest in history: around $2.8 billion in sponsorship, up from $1.8 billion in 2022, and roughly $13 billion across the four-year cycle. But dig underneath the headline and one analysis found something else: the value of the rights per match fell about 19 percent, and the number of broadcast deals dropped about 11 percent, compared with 2022.

Both things are true at once. The overall total went up because there were far more games to sell — 48 teams played 104 matches. Yet each individual game cleared less money than before. That gap is the crucial context for FIFA's push to expand again, to 64 teams for 2030: the growth is coming from selling more matches, not from charging more for each one.

It raises a real question about whether football's biggest event is nearing a ceiling on what any single match is actually worth.

The question The World Cup made more money overall but less per game. Is FIFA simply selling more matches to keep the total rising — and if the per-game price keeps sliding, how many more teams can it add before bigger stops meaning richer?
Story 05

United sells a stadium that isn't built.

Source · Inside Sport / The Athletic · July 2026

Manchester United has opened talks to sell the naming rights to a planned 100,000-seat stadium — one that has not yet been built. The club describes naming rights as "an important revenue stream" and is looking for partners well ahead of construction.

This is a club raising money against a building that is still only a drawing. Naming rights, hospitality and other future income can be pre-sold and then used to help pay for the build itself — turning the stadium project into a financing tool before a single brick is laid. It is a template that other clubs planning expensive new grounds, Newcastle among them, will be watching closely.

The upside is obvious: money in early lowers the risk of a huge project. The catch is that every pound promised now is a pound the finished stadium has to deliver later.

The question United is monetising a stadium years before it opens. Does pre-selling the future genuinely help fund and de-risk the build — or does promising tomorrow's revenue today just load more pressure onto a project that then has to deliver?
Story 06

FIFA's record payout reaches the clubs.

Source · FIFA / Off The Pitch · July 2026

FIFA confirmed a record $355 million payout to clubs whose players featured at the World Cup — its "Club Benefits Programme." That is up about 70 percent from the $209 million paid after Qatar 2022. For the first time, the money also reaches clubs that released players for qualifying matches, at roughly $11,000 per player per day.

Clubs, not FIFA, develop and pay the players the tournament depends on, and they have long complained they see too little of the money it generates. Widening the payout to qualifying clubs deepens the financial link between the World Cup and the thousands of clubs that feed it — and, conveniently, buys goodwill just as FIFA pushes an ever-bigger, ever-more-crowded international calendar that leans harder on those same players.

The generosity and the demands are two sides of the same coin: more matches mean more money to share, but also more strain on the clubs being asked to share their players.

The question FIFA is sharing more of its record haul with clubs than ever before. Is this a genuine rebalancing toward the clubs that supply the talent — or a down-payment to keep them onside as the international calendar keeps growing?
