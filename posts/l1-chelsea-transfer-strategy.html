A look into Chelsea's 'unique' transfer strategy.

Chelsea's transfer business has consistently raised eyebrows since the change of ownership in 2022. After another change at the top of the club, we're left wondering: what does the future hold for Chelsea?

Written by Marcus Puhalj
Read
7 min
Published
19 Oct 2026

CASE STUDY

01 2022: changes of ownership and structures

Roman Abramovich's departure from Chelsea in 2022 meant a lot more than simply a change in ownership. He was a figurehead for one of the most successful ownership eras in modern football. The sanctions placed on him and the club by the British Government signified the end of his tenure at Chelsea and the removal of a structure that, for nearly two decades, had become synonymous with the club's identity. Chelsea had operated with a relatively established sporting hierarchy, but the transition to Todd Boehly, Mark Walter, Clearlake Capital, and their wider consortium initially produced anything but continuity and assurance.

Boehly's first months were particularly unconventional. Following Marina Granovskaia's departure, he temporarily took responsibility of sporting operations while the new ownership rushed to sew together their fabric in the boardroom. The result was an extraordinary opening transfer window in summer of 2022: Chelsea spent frantically while simultaneously changing managers and reshaping the personnel responsible for recruitment. Boehly's interim role as sporting director was made official by Chelsea in June 2022. That initial period therefore looked less like the execution of a finished plan and more like the construction of one in real time. In turn, Chelsea accumulated a remarkably large collection of young players and they were forced to operate with a squad that stretched beyond the Premier League's 25-man senior registration limit. Four years later, however, the question has changed.

What initially looked chaotic now appears to contain a recognizable commercial logic.

02 A 'unique' transfer strategy

Chelsea's transfer strategy under Boehly and Clearlake has been built around three interconnected ideas: time, youth, and volume.

The implementation of unusually long player contracts was a canonic development at the club under the new ownership. Chelsea slowly became known for signing very expensive young and largely unproven players to unprecedented contracts lasting six, seven, and even eight years. The financial attraction was straightforward: under the accounting implementation used for player registrations, a transfer fee is generally spread across the player's contract rather than immediately appearing as one enormous expense. A £70m player on a seven-year contract, for example, would initially create a much smaller annual amortisation charge than the same player signed for three years. That does not make the player cheaper. It changes when the cost is recognized. And the distinction became particularly important for Chelsea because the club was simultaneously acquiring players at enormous volume. UEFA's rules now impose a five-year maximum for amortisation purposes, while the Premier League introduced the same five-year ceiling for new or extended contracts from December 2023.

Their second focus was the age of their new signings. Chelsea consistently targeted players whose careers were in ascension rather than established stars approaching their peak. The theory is essentially an investment thesis applied to football: acquire an asset before its maximum value is reached, develop it, and either let that asset pay itself off with performances on the pitch, or, sell them at a higher price because now that the player has played for one of the biggest clubs in the best league in the world, naturally, the asset's value would've appreciated. Regardless, the club will benefit from the youth of the player in question.

There have already been examples of the second outcome. Ian Maatsen graduated from Chelsea's academy pathway before eventually moving to Aston Villa for around €44.5m; Lewis Hall (academy graduate) was sold to Newcastle for approximately €33m; Conor Gallagher (academy graduate) went to Atlético Madrid for about €42m; and Omari Hutchinson was sold to Ipswich for approximately €23.5m. To the delight of the Chelsea board, academy graduates' transfer fees are essentially pure profit because you never had to pay a transfer fee for them in the first place as they climbed up the club's youth ranks.

This leads into the third element: volume. Chelsea haven't assembled a regular sized squad over the past years, the number of first team players they've had at the club at the same time has been nothing short of ridiculous. To put it simply: their squad looks like an investment portfolio. The sheer volume of players creates opportunities that simply do not exist within a conventional squad structure, allowing Chelsea to move players around, develop their value, and create potential markets for them beyond Stamford Bridge. It is this broader network of movement — rather than simply the number of players Chelsea have signed — that makes their approach particularly distinctive. The clearest examples of this are present across their business within the wider BlueCo structure. Chelsea and Strasbourg (sister club in Ligue 1 France, owned by the BlueCo consortium) completed 12 transactions during the 2025-26 season alone, with players moving between the clubs through permanent transfers and loans. Meanwhile, Chelsea completed eight player transactions across four years with Brighton and Aston Villa of the Premier League, more than any other Premier League clubs during that period. This is where Chelsea's model begins to resemble player trading rather than traditional squad building. A player can be purchased, developed, loaned, reassessed, moved permanently or leveraged through a future player signing. The club does not need every signing to become a first-team star for the investment to produce value. Chelsea have been effectively betting that enough players will appreciate in value to compensate for those who do not.

03 Nobody likes inflation… right?

If Chelsea's squad was an investment portfolio, the 2026 summer transfer window was the moment when a significant portion of that portfolio cashed in. The numbers are unprecedented. Chelsea moved 39 players out of the club during the summer: 17 on permanent transfers and another 22 on loan. Even after this enormous exodus, the club's first-team listing still contains 30 players. And this summer they generated a transfer profit for the first time since the change of ownership 4 years ago. BBC Sport's calculations placed Chelsea's spending at approximately £349m and outgoing permanent-transfer income at around £382m. Chelsea's own internal calculations, which also account for loan fees and future obligations, put the value generated from departures above £500m and estimated the overall profit from transfer activity at roughly £120m.

The biggest transactions illustrate why. Enzo Fernández departed for £125m (signed in 2023 for £107m), Nicolas Jackson for £65m (signed in 2023 for £30m), Marc Cucurella for approximately £51.8m (signed in 2022 for £60m), while Liam Delap and Andrey Santos each generated around £50m. Chelsea could therefore sell several players for enormous sums while continuing to invest heavily on their incoming players. But there is another force working in Chelsea's favour: football inflation. The transfer market itself has become dramatically more expensive.

Across the seven departures Chelsea had paid a fee for, the club paid about £257m and sold for about £357m: a premium of roughly £100m, or 39 per cent. Three more, two academy graduates and a free signing, brought in a further £64m at no transfer cost.

Today's Premier League is operating in an environment where nine-figure transfer fees have moved from extraordinary exceptions towards an increasingly familiar part of the elite market. The 2026 summer transfer window identifies an acceleration in player values with the £100m threshold becoming increasingly normal rather than exceptional. Chelsea's model is particularly well positioned to exploit that inflation because they accumulated so many young players before their eventual market values (inflation aside) were fully established. A player purchased for £20m or £30m does not need to become one of the world's best footballers to generate a substantial accounting or commercial return if the market subsequently values him at £50m or £60m (take Liam Delap and Andrey Santos as examples). As a result of this, Chelsea were able to spread £349m across 11 new players to their first team squad and still turned a profit of over £100m. As it turns out, what was at first a very scattered approach has come to be a considerably more coherent model when we are able to see the entire life cycle of the players' time at the club. The danger, however, is that footballers are not stocks. Their value can collapse through injury, poor performance, contractual complications or simply a failure to find a buyer. The strategy therefore depends on Chelsea being able to continually identify talent, develop it, and find markets willing to pay more than the club's remaining financial commitment.

For now, the inflation of the football market has made that gamble look considerably more attractive.

04 The contrarian read: will their legacy live on?

The irony of Chelsea's 2026 summer is that the clearest evidence in support of the recent transfer strategy arrived just as two of the people most closely associated with its creation were preparing to leave. In September, Todd Boehly and Mark Walter sold their stakes to Clearlake Capital, giving Clearlake full control of Chelsea while Boehly stepped down as chairman. That creates an obvious question: was the extraordinary trading activity of the past four years a product of Boehly and Walter's ownership philosophy, or has it become embedded deeply enough within Chelsea's structure to survive them?

The answer may depend on how Clearlake handles business in summer 2027.

If the 2026 window is viewed simply as the final clean-up of an oversized squad/mistakes left behind from their American ex-owners, Chelsea could begin moving towards a more conventional recruitment model: fewer signings, greater emphasis on established players and a smaller group of assets to manage. After all, Chelsea is one of the biggest clubs in England and Europe, they want to be recruiting more established players and build a squad that's able to compete for trophies year in year out. The fans will not accept anything less for too much longer. But there is always the possibility that Chelsea will now continue gathering huge collections of young players that can eventually be converted into significant transfer income. It has produced a financial return at precisely the moment when football's transfer market has become more inflated than ever. That does not prove the model is sustainable. It does, however, make it considerably harder to dismiss.
