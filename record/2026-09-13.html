The Record · Issue 13 · 13 Sep 2026

What the money is actually buying.

Ten investment funds are bidding for a slice of Serie A’s overseas television rights at a valuation of around €4bn, against about €250m of revenue a season. Brazil’s federation sold its cup rights centrally for the first time and the price went up 80%. The Premier League proposed something similar with its advertising and six clubs said no. Turkish Airlines paid what Liverpool call a Premier League record for their shirt front in the same week Just Eat left the Champions League a year early. Inter played at Real Madrid with a blank shirt. And Bordeaux lost their rescue investor, because what the fund was really buying was a place in a league that nobody could grant.

Story 01

Ten funds want a piece of Serie A’s overseas rights. They are paying for a plan.

Source · Calcio e Finanza / Il Sole 24 Ore · September 2026

Serie A sells its matches outside Italy through a separate company. It is now selling 10–15% of that company, and J.P. Morgan has narrowed the bidding to around ten funds: General Atlantic, Bain, Apollo, Carlyle with Nextalia, Oaktree, Partners Group, Mark Attanasio, Bright Path, FSI and Clessidra. The whole business is valued at roughly €4bn. It earns €250–260m a season from foreign broadcasters, and about €209m of that is profit.

€4bn is about nineteen times a year’s profit. That is a high price for a business growing slowly, and a normal one for a business about to double. The sale comes with a plan to double it: €500–520m a season within ten years, with new offices in Latin America, Asia and Africa. So the buyers are not paying for what Serie A earns abroad today. They are paying for the plan. And it is the clubs, not the fund, who would have to make the plan work.

Several bidders are reported to be using their credit divisions rather than their equity ones. That usually means a fixed return rather than a share of the upside. J.P. Morgan reports back to the clubs in November, and fourteen of the twenty have to vote it through.

The question The clubs are handing part of their overseas business to a fund in order to grow it. So the question is whether outside money really does deliver a bigger audience abroad and a bigger cheque in ten years — or whether the clubs have simply sold a share of growth they will have to produce themselves anyway.
Story 02

Brazil sold its cup centrally for the first time. The price went up 80%.

Source · Sportcal · September 2026

The Brazilian federation has ended twenty years of Globo exclusivity on the Copa do Brasil. Four packages covering 2027 to 2030 go to Globo, to Amazon Prime Video, and to a third buyer the CBF has not named, reported to be ESPN. The price is BR$1.08bn a year, and more than BR$4bn across the cycle. That is roughly 80% more than before.

The CBF says three of its own decisions produced the increase. It sold the rights centrally for the first time. It gave the biggest matches their own kick-off slots, so they stopped taking viewers from each other. And it took production in house, so all 157 games are now shown. In 2026, when Globo produced the tournament, 55 early-round games were not broadcast at all. Part of the extra money is therefore being paid for matches that did not exist on television before.

The question Brazil has just shown it can sell its cup for 80% more. So the question is what the extra money is spent on — the academies and facilities that make Brazilian players worth buying in the first place, or the wage bills and debts the clubs already carry.
Story 03

The Premier League wants to sell its advertising together. Six clubs said no.

Source · Calcio e Finanza, reporting The Telegraph · September 2026

The Premier League has proposed selling more of its commercial inventory collectively. The main item is pitchside LED advertising, most of which each club currently sells for itself. The league argues the central commercial pot could rise from around £200m a year to £750m. United, City, Liverpool, Arsenal, Chelsea and Spurs are opposed. It returns to the shareholders’ meeting on 24 September.

Central commercial income is small today. Of the £2.8bn the league distributed to clubs in 2024-25, £158m came from it: £7.9m a club, shared equally. Broadcast rights have been sold collectively since 1992. Most commercial rights never have.

The mechanism is simple. Money in the central pot is split twenty ways. Money a club raises from its own advertising boards is not. So pooling that inventory takes value from the clubs who can sell it for the most and spreads it across the other fourteen. The six have not set out their reasoning in public. What is clear is what the change would cost them.

The question Costs are rising across the league and the fourteen need a bigger shared pot more than the six do. So the question is whether the six ever trade some of their own selling power for it — or whether keeping the gap open is worth more to them than the extra money.
Story 04

Liverpool set a shirt-front record. A Champions League sponsor walked out the same week.

Source · Sportcal / SportsPro · September 2026

Turkish Airlines will put its name on the front of Liverpool’s men’s, women’s and academy shirts from 1 June 2027. The deal is reported at more than £300m over five years, which works out at about £60m a season. Standard Chartered, the bank it replaces after seventeen years, was paying around £50m a season. Liverpool had reportedly been seeking around £70m. The club says no Premier League side has been paid more for the front of its shirt; Manchester United say their Snapdragon deal is worth a similar amount a season.

In the same week, Just Eat left its Champions League sponsorship a year early. Its head of sponsorship said on the record that the deal “did not fit the needs of the business” after growth slowed following Covid. Hyundai has taken the slot immediately and holds it to 2030-31. Haier joins from the next cycle. The competition is also adding sponsor slots, from nine to twelve, on six-year deals.

The two buyers want different things from the same shirt. An airline wants to be known in the countries it flies to, and does not have to show that the shirt sold any tickets. A food delivery company has to justify the cost against its profits, and when its growth stopped, the sponsorship stopped with it.

The question Turkish Airlines, Emirates, Etihad, Qatar Airways and Riyadh Air now sit across the top of the shirt market, and a consumer brand has just walked away from the Champions League. So the question is which industries decide football is worth this money next — and whether the buyers holding up prices are companies selling to fans at all.
Story 05

A shirt sponsor pays for a season. In some countries the logo has to come off.

Source · Calcio e Finanza / SBC News · September 2026

Inter played at Real Madrid this month with nothing on the front of their shirt. Spanish law bans betting logos on kit, and UEFA’s rules say the law of the host country applies. Inter’s sponsor is the betting company Betsson. It paid a fine to keep its logo on for a match in Spain last season, and this time it did not. So Betsson paid for a season of Inter shirt fronts and did not get that match.

The same problem is turning up elsewhere for different reasons. France’s gambling regulator is reviewing Robinhood’s shirt deal at Nice, because Robinhood now runs a US prediction-market business, which France classes as gambling and has banned since November 2024. In England, the betting firm Entain wrote to ten Premier League clubs telling them to drop sponsors with no British licence. Sunderland then became the eleventh top-flight club to put an unlicensed operator on its kit, on the sleeve. And Bulgaria is drafting a law that would shrink betting logos on shirts to 10cm square.

The pattern is the same every time. A club sells the front of its shirt as one thing, covering a whole season. Whether the logo can actually be worn depends on the country each match is played in, and those rules keep changing. Betsson lost its place on the shirt for an away tie at Real Madrid. That is one of the most watched matches Inter will play all season, and it is exactly the kind of match a sponsor is paying to appear in.

The question A sponsor can lose the shirt for the biggest games of the season, in the countries where the rules say no. So the question is whether these contracts were priced with that risk built in from the start — or whether the club ends up paying for it, in a discount or in something else it has to give the sponsor instead.
Story 06

Bordeaux had the rescue money. It needed a place in a league nobody could give it.

Source · Inside World Football · September 2026

Park Bench, an American fund, was putting together a takeover of Bordeaux with Britain’s Sparta Capital. The investment had one condition: the club had to be readmitted to a national division. On 3 September the French federation refused to place the six-time champions in National 2, saying it had been advised there was no safe legal route to do it. The condition failed and the fund walked away.

The money was not the problem. Park Bench and Sparta had already helped cover the club’s overdue wages, and had €10.6m sitting in escrow with a further $10m held at JPMorgan. Bordeaux are under a court-approved plan that cut their debts from about €94m to roughly €26m. At Régional 1, where they now play, the club cannot earn enough to pay even that.

The condition tells you how the deal was priced. A club in a national division earns broadcast and sponsorship money, and is worth more to the next buyer. The same club in regional football is not. Park Bench was not paying for the squad or the ground. It was paying for the division the club would be put back into — and the division is not the club’s to sell. Only the federation can grant it, and it declined.

The question Park Bench was paying for the division, not for the club. So the question is what a club like Bordeaux is worth to any buyer, when a federation can simply say no to the one thing that gives it value.
