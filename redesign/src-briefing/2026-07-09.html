The rules switch on.

A quiet mid-World-Cup week, but the money moved: England's regulator went live, the leveraged multi-club model kept unwinding, and capital kept buying football's rights and cashflows rather than its clubs.

Story 01

England's football regulator switches on.

Source · Global Regulation Tomorrow / Norton Rose Fulbright · 1 July 2026

On 1 July, England's Independent Football Regulator published its final licensing rules. From now on, every club in the top five tiers of English football — about 116 clubs, from the Premier League down to the National League — will need a licence to play. Clubs can apply from 1 November 2026, the window closes on 26 February 2027, and all of them must be licensed before the 2027/28 season.

This matters because it changes who holds the power. For the first time, an outside body decides whether an owner is fit to run an English club and whether that club's finances are sound. A rulebook that has been talked about for years is now live. Owners face a formal test; clubs face scrutiny and paperwork they have never had before.

The regulator exists because of the Football Governance Act 2025, itself a reaction to near-collapses like Bury and the failed European Super League. The rules are written. What is untested is how hard the regulator will actually push.

The question The rulebook is now live, but no owner has been tested against it yet. When the first real case arrives, does the regulator show it has teeth — blocking or forcing changes on an owner — or does it wave most applicants through and settle into box-ticking?
Story 02

The multi-club model keeps unwinding.

Source · ESPN, Insider Sport, Fox Sports, City A.M. · late June–early July 2026

Four versions of the same story landed this week. LAFC, the celebrity-backed Los Angeles club, sold 96.5% of Switzerland's Grasshopper Zürich. Botafogo, owned by John Textor's Eagle Football, is in court-supervised restructuring under about R$2.7bn (roughly $540m) of debt. Sevilla's €440m takeover fronted by Sergio Ramos fell apart after the buyers halved their offer. And City Football Group handed its Japanese club, Yokohama F. Marinos, to Nissan.

Multi-club ownership, or MCO, means one company owning several clubs across different countries, so it can share players, scouting and costs. Much of the first wave was built on borrowed money and famous names. High interest rates and messy cross-border rules have made that model expensive to fund and hard to run. This week's retreat suggests it was oversold.

Even City Football Group — the group that turned multi-club ownership into an industry — is now trimming its collection rather than adding to it. That is the clearest signal yet that the land-grab phase is over.

The question The leveraged, celebrity-backed version of multi-club ownership is plainly in retreat. Does the whole idea now shrink back — or do patient, deep-pocketed owners simply buy up the clubs the borrowers are dropping, and quietly build the next generation of networks?
Story 03

Serie A shops its overseas TV rights.

Source · SportsPro / Private Equity Wire · early July 2026

Italy's top league is in talks with private-equity firms — Apollo, CVC, Ares and Sixth Street among the names linked — about selling up to 49% of the business that sells Serie A matches abroad. JP Morgan is advising. That overseas unit brings in around €250m a year, far behind what England and Spain earn from foreign markets.

The appeal is simple: the league gets a large cash injection now without giving up control of its matches at home. Selling only a minority of just the international arm raises money while the clubs keep their domestic rights. Spain's LaLiga did a version of this with CVC; Germany's Bundesliga clubs rejected the idea twice.

The catch is the vote. Serie A needs 14 of its 20 clubs to agree, and a similar plan in 2021 collapsed on exactly that math. Bigger clubs often resist because they would rather keep future upside than take cash today.

The question Serie A wants outside money for its overseas rights without losing control at home. Does it get 14 clubs over the line this time — or does it stall again, as in 2021, and watch the revenue gap to England and Spain keep widening?
Story 04

A media fund buys Swansea — as content.

Source · PR Newswire · 3–4 July 2026

Shamrock Capital, a $7.4bn Los Angeles firm best known for owning Taylor Swift's back catalogue, took a stake in the Championship club Swansea City. The twist is how it invested: through its content arm, treating the club as an undervalued media and streaming property rather than a football bet. Terms were not disclosed.

Most owners buy a club for trophies, or to buy and sell players at a profit. Shamrock is betting that the real value sits in the footage, documentaries, archive and licensing — the intellectual property a club generates. It is a genuinely new reason to own a football team, and if it works, others will copy it.

The open question is whether the numbers hold. A mid-table English second-tier club has to throw off a lot of content and licensing income to satisfy an investment fund's return targets.

The question Shamrock is treating a second-tier club as a media library, not a team. Does the content actually pay — enough streaming and licensing income to hit a fund's return target — or is "undervalued IP" a story that outruns the P&L?
Story 05

Buying the cash, not the club.

Source · Sportico / Sportcal · 3 July 2026

Pau Gasol's investment vehicle, Gasol16 Ventures, put €55m into Spain's women's league, Liga F. In return it takes 35–49% of the participating clubs' future commercial income, running all the way to 2050/51. About €40m goes to the clubs and €12m to the league. Four of the 16 clubs, including Real Madrid, chose not to take part.

This is not buying a club. It is buying a slice of future revenue — closer to how a bank turns a stream of income into an asset it can sell. The investor gets football's cashflows without the risk and daily grind of running a team. It is also the largest private investment in women's football so far, a bet that the game's commercial value keeps climbing.

It fits a pattern running through the whole week: money is increasingly interested in football's income, not its trophies.

The question Investors are increasingly buying football's income rather than its clubs. Does this revenue-rights model become the standard way to fund the women's game — or do the clubs that opted out, like Real Madrid, prove that selling 25 years of upside is a deal owners come to regret?
Story 06

The World Cup's record haul.

Source · SportsPro / Ampere Analysis · early July 2026

The 2026 World Cup, now under way across the United States, Canada and Mexico, is on track to be the most commercially lucrative standalone event ever staged. FIFA sold all 16 of its global sponsor slots — a first — and sponsorship income alone is projected at roughly $2.4bn to $2.8bn, up around 37% on 2022. Total revenue for the cycle is heading toward $13bn.

The bigger 48-team format, more matches and the wealthy North American market have lifted the ceiling. That matters beyond the tournament: this is now the benchmark against which every league and club deal is measured. It also shows where the money comes from — US brands and Gulf money, with Saudi Arabia's PIF among the official supporters, now supply much of it.

FIFA is also testing demand-based ticket pricing for the first time, which has pushed some seats past $4,000 — squeezing more from fans as well as sponsors.

The question The bigger tournament has delivered record sponsorship and ticket revenue. Does the 48-team format prove a permanent step up in value — or has FIFA stretched the calendar and fans' wallets far enough that the next cycle brings pushback?
