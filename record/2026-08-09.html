Red ink, fresh capital all around.

Six stories from a week when English football’s clubs booked a record loss — even as private capital pushed deeper into everything around them: the agents who represent the players, the arms that sell the rights, the broadcasters, and the leagues rewriting who is even allowed to own in.

Story 01

English football’s clubs just lost nearly a billion pounds.

Source · Deloitte / ESPN · August 2026

Deloitte’s annual review of football finance found that Premier League clubs made a combined pre-tax loss of about £948 million in 2024-25, up from £135 million the year before. That is close to a sevenfold jump in a single season. Wages and the cost of paying off transfer fees rose faster than income, and there were fewer big player sales to paper over the gap.

The timing is pointed. This is the first season the league has swapped its old profitability rules — PSR — for a new “squad cost ratio” that ties spending to revenue. The £948m figure is the problem those rules are meant to fix, and the evidence that, so far, the curve is still bending the wrong way.

A record loss does not mean the clubs are in danger; owners keep funding them and revenues remain the highest in the game. But it sets the backdrop for everything else this week. The more clubs lose, the more the money — and the control — shifts to whoever is willing to fund them.

The question New spending rules arrive in the same season losses hit a record. Will tying costs to revenue actually slow the bleeding, or does it simply lock in the gap between the clubs that earn the most and everyone chasing them?
Story 02

Private equity buys the agents, too.

Source · Sportico · August 2026

Providence Equity is finishing a deal to take full control of Wasserman’s agency business — now branded The Team — buying out founder Casey Wasserman’s remaining stake at a valuation near $3.4 billion. Providence already owned about 60%; this hands a single private-equity firm sole ownership of one of the largest player-representation books in sport, ahead of interest from UTA, Permira and others.

Football’s money has spent years buying clubs, leagues and rights. This is capital buying the layer in between — the agents who represent the players and broker the transfers. One fund now sits on that side of the market, with an interest in the fees that flow every time a player moves.

It is a quiet but telling shift. The people who advise players are becoming assets themselves, owned by the same kind of investors buying everything else in the game.

The question With a private-equity firm now owning a major agency outright, who does a player’s agent ultimately answer to — the player across the table, or the fund that owns the agency?
Story 03

A Gulf broadcaster walks away from France.

Source · TechTimes / Ampere · August 2026

From 15 August, LaLiga’s matches in France will be shown by Disney+ and DAZN, ending beIN Sports’ 14-year hold on the Spanish league there. It is the second big French rights property the Qatari-owned broadcaster has let go, after already stepping back from Ligue 1.

beIN was built to plant Qatar’s flag in Western sport, and France was one of its anchor markets. Walking away is not one lost auction — it is a state-backed broadcaster deciding a flagship market is no longer worth the price, and ceding the ground to American streamers.

For now the gap is being filled: Disney+ and DAZN are the ones stepping in. But that is not the same as those prices holding. It is worth remembering DAZN has already tried and failed at exactly this in France — its four-year Ligue 1 deal collapsed and it retreated inside twelve months. A state broadcaster can carry a flagship market at a loss for strategic reasons; a commercial streamer walks from rights that do not pay their way.

The question DAZN has already walked away from French football once. If the streamers cannot make these rights pay the way a state broadcaster could, does beIN’s retreat mark the start of French football’s TV money falling?
Story 04

Serie A shops its overseas TV rights.

Source · SportsPro / Goal · August 2026

Serie A, working with JP Morgan, has quietly approached private-equity firms — Apollo, CVC, Ares and Sixth Street among them — about selling up to 49% of the arm that sells its matches outside Italy. That overseas business brings in only about €250 million a year, a fraction of what the Premier League and LaLiga earn abroad.

The logic is to take cash now against future rights income, rather than wait and hope the overseas number grows on its own. It is the same move CVC made buying into LaLiga’s commercial business — applied this time to just the international-TV slice.

Serie A is not alone. Germany’s Bundesliga is in talks over a roughly €1 billion injection from a US investment firm to fund overseas tours and international marketing, and close the same gap on the Premier League — a softer, central-marketing route it turned to after German clubs rejected an outright sale of a media-rights stake. Different structures, one motive: mid-tier leagues reaching for outside money to catch a rival pulling away.

It is the gap that now defines European football’s middle tier. Leagues that cannot match the Premier League’s foreign income are increasingly willing to sell a piece of tomorrow’s rights, or take an investor’s cash today, to close the gap.

The question Selling a stake or taking an injection brings money in now but hands a share of the future — or a claim on it — to an outside investor. Does that capital help Serie A and the Bundesliga catch up, or lock in their second-tier position while the buyer takes the upside?
Story 05

Two buyers, one stake, one club.

Source · Bloomberg / Sky Sports · August 2026

Amanda Staveley — the dealmaker behind Newcastle’s 2021 takeover — has agreed to buy the Gold family’s 25.1% stake in West Ham for about £150 million, backed by a Chicago investment firm. The catch: Daniel Křetínský, who already owns 27% of the club, had a deal in place for those same shares.

That sets up a genuine ownership fight. Křetínský already owns 27%; adding the Golds’ 25.1% on top would push him past half the club and into outright control. Staveley agreeing to buy the same shares is a move to stop that. But the Golds cannot simply hand-pick a buyer: West Ham’s shareholder agreement gives the existing owners a right of first refusal — a “pre-emption” right — meaning an insider like Křetínský may be able to match Staveley’s price and take the shares himself.

So her agreement is the opening move in a contest, not a settled deal. The point is that the shareholder rules — as much as the highest bid — may decide who actually ends up with the shares, and whether Křetínský is allowed to complete his march to control. Even outside the Premier League, English clubs still draw this kind of fight.

The question Is West Ham better off with one controlling owner who can bankroll a quick return to the Premier League — or with a split boardroom where rival investors keep each other in check?
Story 06

A big league closes its doors for good.

Source · Inside World Football · August 2026

Mexico’s Liga MX has written promotion and relegation out of its rules for good and split from the national federation over the issue. From 2026-27 the top flight is a closed set of clubs — the only way in is to buy an existing licence — and payments to second-tier clubs end.

This is the clearest move yet by a major football league toward the American model. In an open league you earn your place by results; in a closed one the set of clubs is fixed and a place becomes a permanent, tradable asset — like owning an NFL franchise. Club membership itself turns into something that can be bought and sold, rather than won on the pitch.

The obvious audience is US investors, who know that model well and have been circling Mexican football. Relegation is the risk they dislike most: a club that drops down loses its TV and matchday income overnight and its value collapses. Close the league and that trapdoor disappears — each club becomes a cleaner, more predictable asset to buy and to finance.

For how this closed, franchise model differs from football’s open pyramid — and why US capital keeps pushing the game toward it — our analysis is here →

The question A closed league makes clubs safer to invest in but strips out the jeopardy that makes football compelling. Does turning places into franchises bring in the capital Liga MX wants — or cost it the competitive drama that gives the league its value, and dull the pressure that pushes clubs to develop talent?
