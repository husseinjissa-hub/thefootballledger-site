Jorge Mendes stopped negotiating for Ronaldo. The network kept growing.

For two decades, football's most powerful agent was defined by one client. When Cristiano Ronaldo took his contracts elsewhere in 2022, the business did not shrink. What Mendes built was never a client list. It was a network with a seat on more than one side of the deal, and the rules that allowed those seats are now closing.

In January 2023, Cristiano Ronaldo signed for Al-Nassr on a contract reported at £175m a year. The man who negotiated it was not Jorge Mendes. For almost twenty years Mendes had been Ronaldo's agent, and godfather to one of his children. The agency relationship ended late in 2022, after Mendes failed to find him a Champions League club and Ronaldo gave the interview that ended his Manchester United career. The Saudi deal was handled instead by Ronaldo's long-time personal manager, Ricky Regufe. Losing the mandate on the richest contract in the sport should have hurt. It barely registered, because Mendes never built a client list. He built a network, and he owned pieces of it.

The first stake

Mendes founded Gestifute in 1996, after a lower-division playing career and a spell running a video shop and a nightclub in the northern town of Caminha. The founding matters less than what he did seven years later. Portugal, unlike most football countries, allowed outside parties to own part of a player's economic rights: a share of the fee when he is next sold.

In July 2003, Porto paid Gestifute €2.25m for 20 per cent of Deco's rights, plus small slices of Ricardo Carvalho and Paulo Ferreira. Soon after, Gestifute sold Porto a further 15 per cent of Deco and took a stake in the striker Benni McCarthy as part of the payment.

That was the turn. A normal agent earns a commission when a player moves. Mendes now held a piece of the player, and pieces of the next ones, traded with the club like shares. He was no longer only representing the asset. He partly owned it. Every later chapter is a bigger version of the same idea: be on more than one side of the deal.

How the network was built

From there, each relationship added a different kind of leverage, and none of them depended on the others.

  • Ronaldo, the brand. Mendes brokered his move from Manchester United to Real Madrid in 2009 for €94m, a world record at the time. It made Mendes famous far beyond Portugal.
  • Mourinho, the coaches. He placed José Mourinho at Inter in 2008 and at Real Madrid in 2010, on a four-year contract reported at £40m. A coach who trusts your players is a buyer.
  • The Portuguese clubs, the supply. Benfica, Porto and Sporting survive by selling talent, and Mendes sat in the middle of that trade. João Félix's €126m move from Benfica to Atlético Madrid in 2019 is still the biggest sale in Portuguese football.
  • Wolves, the club. In January 2016 a Fosun subsidiary bought 20 per cent of Start, the holding company that owns Gestifute. Six months later Fosun bought Wolves for £45m, after taking Mendes's advice. By 2022, Wolves had reportedly signed 22 of his clients.

The Wolves link shows the model most clearly. In an email later published by Der Spiegel, the club's chairman, Jeff Shi, described the investment as mainly about "our bet and trust on Jorge." An owner held shares in the agency, the agency supplied the players and the coach, and the club was the showroom. Mendes was not a star with a client list. He was the hub everyone else had to route through.

The test

If Mendes's power had rested on Ronaldo, the split should have shown up in the numbers. It did not. Gestifute sits inside Start SGPS alongside Polaris Sports, which manages players' image rights, and two smaller units. In 2022, Ronaldo's last year as a client, group profit doubled to €32.7m, with adjusted EBITDA of €44m, according to accounts reported by Forbes Portugal. After he left, the value of his business held up. Forbes Portugal builds each list mainly on the previous year's company accounts, so its 2023 list reflects 2022, Ronaldo's last year as a client. The 2024 and 2025 lists rest on 2023 and 2024, the first two years without him. They valued Mendes's business holdings at €411m and €407m, up from €373m.

The deals show what kept the value up. In the summer of 2023, the first window after the split, at least 11 of his clients changed clubs, according to Goal. Gonçalo Ramos, Manuel Ugarte and Bradley Barcola went to Paris Saint-Germain, and Matheus Nunes joined Manchester City for €62m. Rúben Neves and Fabinho moved to Saudi Arabia, six months after Ronaldo had gone there without Mendes.

The test is not perfectly clean. Ronaldo left the agency, not every Mendes company: Forbes Portugal reported in 2024 that his image-rights contract with Polaris was still active, so some Ronaldo income kept flowing into the group. What Mendes lost was the agency mandate, meaning the commission on every contract Ronaldo signs from here on, starting with the richest one in the sport. That is a real loss, and the value of the business held up through it. The split does not prove Ronaldo was worth nothing to Mendes. It shows the business did not need Ronaldo's deals to keep growing.

The limits

The network has limits, and regulators have found most of them. Each one targets the same thing: being on more than one side of the deal.

  • The first stake is now illegal. FIFA banned third-party ownership of players' economic rights worldwide in 2015, closing the mechanism that first made Mendes an owner as well as an agent.
  • The showroom is weaker. In April 2026 Wolves were relegated from the Premier League. The club that best displayed the model is now playing in the Championship.
  • Acting for several sides is back in question. FIFA's 2023 agent rules cap agents' fees and ban an agent from representing more than one party in most deals. Both were suspended at the end of 2023 after legal challenges. In July 2026 the EU's top court ruled that rules like these can in principle be justified, but left German courts to decide whether FIFA's actually are. FIFA has called the ruling a win and wants a new system in place by January 2027.
  • The tax case. In May 2025, Portugal's public prosecutor accused Mendes and his wife of tax fraud in an investigation called Operation Offside, over the transfer of nearly half of one of his companies to her. It does not concern player transfers. Mendes agreed to pay the €18m in question while contesting it, and has not been convicted.

Put together, the limits point at one thing. Mendes's edge was never only who he knew. It was where he sat: on the player's side, beside the owner, and for a time on the side that owned the player's value. His relationships will survive the rule changes. The seats that made those relationships so profitable may not.

Where it goes

The obvious question about Mendes is succession. He turned 60 in January, and the network was built around him. But football has already run that experiment. When Mino Raiola died in April 2022, his partner Rafaela Pimenta held the agency together while rivals tried to poach its clients, and in January 2025 she negotiated Erling Haaland's nine-and-a-half-year contract at Manchester City. An agency with a working bench can outlive its founder. Gestifute has a bench, and its pipeline still flows: in April 2025, Porto's 17-year-old Rodrigo Mora joined Polaris.

The harder question is what replaces the seats. Rivals are buying theirs with capital. In July, Providence Equity agreed to take full control of THE·TEAM, the agency formerly called Wasserman, in a deal reported to value it at $3.4bn. Mendes built his by hand, through player stakes, an owner who invested in his holding company, and a club that bought his players. The stakes are banned, the club is in the Championship, and acting for several sides of a deal may soon be restricted again.

Sitting on more than one side of the deal was Mendes's first moat, and the rules are closing it. The strategic question is not whether Gestifute can survive without Mendes; Raiola's agency showed that it can. It is whether Gestifute is building a second moat that works inside the new rules: a capital partner on the scale of the private-equity agencies, a club relationship to replace a relegated Wolves, and first claim on Portugal's next generation. The first transfer windows under FIFA's new system, which it wants in place by January 2027, will start to answer it.
What to watch
  • FIFA's agent rules:whether German courts accept the fee cap and the limits on representing several parties, and what FIFA's January 2027 system keeps.
  • Wolves in the Championship:whether the club keeps buying through Gestifute, and whether Fosun stays as owner.
  • Forbes Portugal's 2026 list:whether the valuation of his business holdings holds around €400m on 2025 accounts, a third year without Ronaldo's deals.
  • Operation Offside:the outcome of the €18m tax case, and whether any wider investigation touches Gestifute's football business.
  • The private-equity agencies:whether capital-backed rivals start winning the Portuguese talent that has always gone through Mendes.
