Story 01

Manchester City booked £949.94m as Abu Dhabi sponsorship. The sponsors paid £119.25m of it.

Source · Premier League / Calcio e Finanza / Sky Sports / ESPN / Bloomberg · 29 September – 2 October 2026

Football’s financial rules treat two kinds of money differently. Income a club earns, such as sponsorship, counts towards what it is allowed to spend. Money an owner puts in has, for most of the past fifteen years, been limited or excluded. That distinction is what the case against Manchester City has always been about. City are owned by Abu Dhabi United Group, the investment vehicle of Sheikh Mansour, and many of their biggest sponsors are Abu Dhabi companies.

Last week’s Briefing reported, citing The Athletic, that an independent commission had found City in breach of almost all of the 115 Premier League charges brought in 2023. On 29 September the Premier League published a redacted version of the decision. It confirms the commission found City guilty of all the financial charges covering 2009-10 to 2017-18, and upheld three of the four charges that City failed to cooperate with the investigation. The league says sham arrangements were used to inflate revenue and reduce costs by more than £900m. Its chief executive, Richard Masters, said the club “systematically broke Premier League Rules for nearly a decade”. City lodged an appeal on 1 October and say the decision contains “clear material errors of law, principle and fact”. No sanction has been set; that will be decided at a separate hearing.

Calcio e Finanza, the Italian financial outlet, has read the decision and reproduced a year-by-year table from it. Across the nine seasons:

  • City booked £949.94m of income from Abu Dhabi sponsors.
  • The sponsors themselves paid £119.25m of that.
  • The remaining £830.69m was paid by Abu Dhabi United Group, City’s owner. The decision calls these payments “Tagged Sums”.
  • Separately, £24.5m from an image-rights arrangement was booked as operating income, and £49.4m of operating costs was kept off City’s books.

This is the mechanism in concrete terms. On those figures, for every pound of Abu Dhabi sponsorship in City’s accounts, about 13p came from the sponsors and about 87p from the owner. Booked as sponsorship, the owner’s money counted as income the club had earned. Booked as what it was, it would have counted as owner funding, which the financial rules in force for most of that period limited.

The published decision redacts the sponsors’ names. Etihad, Abu Dhabi’s state airline and City’s principal sponsor, says it is not named, but it has responded anyway. It “categorically rejects” any suggestion that it was involved in improper commercial arrangements, says it will “seek the relevant legal counsel on the options and recourse available to protect its interests”, and says the Premier League “must take responsibility for the consequences”. Asahi, another City sponsor, says it is monitoring developments. Bloomberg reports that City’s chairman, Khaldoon Al Mubarak, who also runs the Abu Dhabi state fund Mubadala, met a UK minister on 14 September to discuss UAE investment in Britain, and that Emirati officials have since warned that a harsh decision could reduce future investment. City were not directly discussed, according to the report.

The case is no longer only about one club’s accounts. The finding says owner money was passed through sponsors. One of those sponsors is a state-owned company now talking about legal action against the league, and the state behind it is a large investor in the country where the league operates. The money itself did not stay inside City either. It paid for the players and the squad that won titles, and those title races and stars are part of what the Premier League sells to broadcasters around the world. The league is now enforcing its rules against spending that, arguably, also helped build its own product.

The question The finding has hurt Manchester City. Will it also hurt the Premier League, which arguably benefited from the same Abu Dhabi money? The league gains credibility by enforcing its rules, but the spending it has ruled against helped make City part of the product it sells, and Etihad and Emirati officials are now warning of consequences. So the question is whether the Premier League comes out of this as a stronger regulator of its own clubs, or pays a price of its own for punishing investment it once profited from.
Story 02

Juventus asks for up to €250m, its seventh capital increase since 2007.

Source · Juventus / Calcio e Finanza / Sky Sport Italia · 29 September – 3 October 2026

Juventus is one of the few big European clubs listed on a stock exchange. When it runs short of money it can hold a capital increase: it issues new shares, existing shareholders are offered them first, and the cash goes to the club. Its controlling shareholder is Exor, the Agnelli family’s holding company, which owns 65.4% of the shares and about 78% of the votes. When Exor “backstops” a capital increase, it promises to buy any shares other investors do not take.

The accounts for the year to June 2026, published on 29 September, explain why another one is needed. Revenue fell 10.4%, from €529.6m to €474.7m. The loss was €66.0m, after €58.1m the year before. Net debt rose from €280.2m to €331.1m. Juventus reached the Champions League last season but went out in the knockout play-off to Galatasaray, and this season it plays in the Europa League. The club expects another loss this year.

The same evening the board proposed a capital increase of up to €250m, in one or more tranches. Exor will advance €60m now and backstop the rest. Shareholders vote on 3 November, and the raise is due to be completed by December. The next morning the shares fell more than 10%, to below €1.68, valuing the whole club at about €777m.

This is not new. On Calcio e Finanza’s count, Juventus has raised capital six times before:

  • 2007: €104.8m
  • 2011-12: €120m
  • 2019-20: €300m
  • 2021-22: €400m
  • 2023-24: €200m
  • November 2025: €98m

That is about €1.22bn in all, of which Exor and its predecessor IFIL supplied about €770m. If Exor takes up its share of the new raise, its total would pass €900m, on the same outlet’s figures. At the price the market set on 30 September, the club was worth less than the cash its shareholders have put in through the six earlier raises, although those figures are in nominal euros spread across nineteen years.

Italy’s other two big clubs show different answers to the same problem. Inter made a €22.7m profit on €518m of revenue. AC Milan, without European football, lost about €24m and borrowed more (see Story 06). Juventus has missed out on the Champions League’s later rounds and covers the gap by going back to its owner. Each raise has kept the club solvent. The accounts published this week show it is not yet paying its own way.

The question Is the seventh capital increase buying Juventus time to rebuild a business that pays for itself, or is going back to Exor now the business model? Exor’s money keeps the club solvent and lets it plan, but every raise that is not followed by a profit makes the next one more likely. So the question is whether this €250m is the one that closes the gap, or the latest instalment in a subsidy that only ends when Exor decides it should.
Story 03

Netflix says it will always be in the conversation for World Cup rights. US bidders are reportedly budgeting up to $2bn a tournament.

Source · Deadline / CNBC / FIFA / Sports Illustrated · September 2026

In the United States, FIFA sells World Cup television rights by language. Fox has shown the men’s tournament in English since 2018 and Telemundo, owned by NBCUniversal, in Spanish. In 2011 Fox paid about $425m and Telemundo about $600m for the 2018 and 2022 tournaments. Both deals were extended to 2026 without a tender, after FIFA moved the 2022 World Cup in Qatar to the winter. For the 2026 tournament, held in June and July, CNBC reports Fox paid about $485m and NBCUniversal about $600m.

The rights to the 2030 and 2034 men’s tournaments are now on sale, and FIFA is offering English and Spanish together. In July CNBC reported that media companies were budgeting between $1.5bn and $2bn for the US rights to each tournament, across both languages. It named Netflix, Disney and YouTube as interested and said Amazon could also enter. These are reported budgets, not bids.

Netflix has now said so in public. Speaking at the Financial Times’ Business of Entertainment conference, reported by Deadline on 24 September, Bela Bajaria, its chief content officer, said: “We have a great relationship with [FIFA] because we have the Women’s World Cup and [we’re] having those conversations. We’re always going to be in the conversation with the leagues.” Netflix already holds the US rights to the 2027 and 2031 Women’s World Cups, every match in English and Spanish, agreed in December 2024.

What Netflix buys in sport is specific. Most of its live sport is single events, such as NFL games on Christmas Day and the women’s tournament. Its largest deal, about $5bn over ten years for WWE’s weekly Raw show, is the exception. Bajaria said the streamer wants rights that feel “eventy”. A World Cup is the biggest event in the sport: 104 matches in about five weeks, every four years, with no regular season to commit to in between.

A men’s World Cup would also put Netflix up against the companies that already show football in the US. Fox has the World Cup. Paramount, which owns CBS, holds the US rights to the Champions League until 2030 in a reported $1.5bn deal, and shows it on CBS and its Paramount+ streaming service. FIFA has sold to a streamer before. DAZN paid a reported $1bn for worldwide rights to the 2025 Club World Cup and showed every match free. The difference in the US is what the World Cup has been until now. Fox and Telemundo show most of it on free-to-air television, where anyone with an aerial can watch. A streamer would put it behind a subscription. If the reported budgets hold, FIFA would be choosing between a price well above the roughly $1.1bn the two incumbents are reported to have paid for 2026 and the reach it has had on broadcast television.

The question If Netflix adds the men’s World Cup to the women’s tournaments it already holds, does it become a regular competitor to Fox and Paramount for football in the US, and does that move American viewers from linear television to streaming? Netflix would own the sport’s two biggest international tournaments in the US; the incumbents would lose their largest football event. So the question is whether the World Cup becomes the event that shifts US football viewing to streaming, or whether Netflix stays a buyer of occasional events while the weekly football stays with Fox and Paramount.
Story 04

Europe’s clubs say UEFA’s next cycle will bring in more than €20bn. They have also redrawn who sits on their board.

Source · Calcio e Finanza / Inside World Football / UEFA · 29 September – 1 October 2026

European Football Clubs, the body formerly called the European Club Association, represents more than 900 clubs. It is no longer only a lobby. It co-owns UC3, the joint venture with UEFA that controls the commercial rights to the Champions League, Europa League and Conference League. Last week’s Briefing described the first big deal of that new era, AB InBev’s beer sponsorship, and asked where the extra money from the next cycle would go.

At the EFC’s general assembly in Copenhagen this week, its chairman, Nasser Al-Khelaifi, who is also president of Paris Saint-Germain, gave his answer. He said UC3 will generate more than €20bn over the four seasons from 2027, more than €5bn a season, and that about €2bn of that will go to clubs outside the European competitions and to youth and women’s football. He called it the biggest solidarity fund in world sport. These are projections from the body that sells the rights, not contracts. For comparison, UEFA expects revenue of about €4.4bn for its club competitions this season.

The share going to solidarity is worth checking against today’s rules. At present 10% of the pool goes in solidarity payments, about €440m this season. On Al-Khelaifi’s figures, €2bn of €20bn is also about 10%. The definitions differ, since the new figure includes youth and women’s football, so the two are not like for like. But on its face, a bigger pool with the same share means more solidarity money in euros, and much more prize money for the clubs that play.

The assembly also changed how the EFC is run. After 18 months of consultation, members adopted these reforms:

  • The board shrinks from 35 to 33 club representatives, all with a vote.
  • Between five and seven seats are reserved for associate members, the smaller clubs that make up most of the membership.
  • The subdivision of the biggest clubs keeps 50% of the votes.
  • Officials from Ajax, Lille, Galatasaray, Hibernian and Ludogorets were elected to the board.

Al-Khelaifi says every member now has a direct voice. Inside World Football reports that the vast majority of the 900-plus clubs will remain associate members without voting rights. Both can be true: the smaller clubs gain seats on the board, and the biggest clubs keep half the votes. Al-Khelaifi also said Real Madrid is “in the process of rejoining” the EFC, five years after it left over the Super League. Separately, FIFA and the EFC agreed to split $250m of Club World Cup solidarity money equally between Europe and the rest of the world.

The distinction this story turns on is between how much money there is and who decides where it goes. The pool is growing. The split for the new cycle is for UEFA and the EFC to set through UC3, and inside the EFC the clubs that receive most of the prize money hold half the votes.

The question With more than €20bn projected and the big clubs keeping half the votes, will the new cycle narrow the gap between Europe’s richest clubs and the rest, or widen it? The reforms give smaller clubs more seats, and solidarity money rises in euros. So the question is whether those seats are used to raise solidarity’s share of the pool, or whether a bigger pool split the old way pays the clubs already winning more than ever.
Story 05

Piqué’s holding company made €1.5m. The Kings League underneath it is shrinking to grow.

Source · Calcio e Finanza / Economía Digital / Palco23 / El Debate / El Confidencial Digital · June – October 2026

Gerard Piqué, the former Barcelona defender, owns his businesses through Kerad Holding. The best known are the Kings League, a seven-a-side competition built for streaming that he launched in 2023; FC Andorra, which he took into Spain’s second division in 2022; and Kosmos, the sports-investment firm whose 25-year Davis Cup deal with tennis’s governing body was ended in January 2023, five years in.

Kerad’s 2025 accounts, first reported by Economía Digital and Palco23 in Spain and summarised by Calcio e Finanza on 2 October, show a holding company in modest profit:

  • Net profit of €1.5m, down from €1.8m in 2024: its second profitable year in a row.
  • No ordinary revenue of its own, and financial income of just over €1m, down from €4.1m.
  • Total assets of €64m, almost €53m of it in stakes in group companies.
  • Equity above €40m, and more than €10m of new long-term borrowing.

A holding company’s accounts show what it owns and what it has borrowed. They do not show whether the businesses underneath make money, and the reported figures do not break the €53m down by company.

The Kings League’s own figures are not filed in the same way. Its chief executive, Djamel Agaoua, told La Vanguardia this summer that revenue reached €52m in 2025, up 30%, that the league is not yet profitable, and that it has raised €110m from investors. In June it began cutting about 40 jobs, roughly 30% of its Barcelona workforce, according to El Debate. Activity in Spain pauses until Christmas while the format is rebuilt. The plan is a cross-border competition for clubs from Spain, Italy, France and Germany, with format and calendar not yet set.

The distinction here is between a brand and a business. The Kings League grew on the reach of Piqué and the online creators who front its teams, and revenue has grown each year. What it has not yet shown is a version of itself that earns more than it costs. Cutting staff and merging national leagues into one competition is a bet that a smaller operation with a bigger product can do that.

The question Is the Kings League a media business that has to spread across countries to make money, or a Spanish entertainment product that has to get smaller to pay its way? The cuts and the cross-border plan try to do both at once: fewer people, bigger audience. So the question is whether the new format turns €52m of revenue into a profit, or whether the athlete-founded model works as a brand long before it works as a business.
Story 06

AC Milan is reported to be in talks to return to adidas, days after its sponsorship passed €100m for the first time.

Source · Calcio e Finanza / Tuttosport / AC Milan / Juventus · September – October 2026

A kit supplier makes and sells a club’s shirts and pays the club for the right, usually a fixed fee each year plus royalties on what it sells. It is one of the largest commercial contracts a club signs, and because kit deals run for many years, the price is often set long before the first shirt is made. Milan wore adidas from 1998 to 2018 and has worn Puma since 2018-19. Puma’s deal was renewed in 2022 without a disclosed fee and runs to 30 June 2028. Milan’s 2024-25 accounts show Puma paid about €25.3m.

Calcio e Finanza and Tuttosport report that Milan is negotiating a return to adidas from 2028-29. Nothing has been signed or announced, and the club says Puma remains an important partner until the contract ends. No value has been reported for a new deal.

The report comes days after Milan’s accounts for the year to June 2026. With no European football last season, revenue was €464.6m, down 6% on the year before, and the club lost about €24m after three years of profit. The missing European income is put at about €80m. Net debt rose from about €92m to €145.3m. Against that, sponsorship income passed €100m for the first time, the club said.

Milan’s rivals set the market it will negotiate in, though the published figures are not like for like:

  • Juventus extended its adidas deal in 2025 for ten years from 2027-28, with €408m in fixed fees, about €40.8m a season before royalties and bonuses.
  • Inter renewed with Nike in 2023 to 2031, reported at about €30m a season including variable payments.

A kit deal is priced mainly on a club’s reach and shirt sales, and its fixed fee is paid whether or not the club qualifies for Europe, though many contracts add bonuses for success. For a club whose accounts have just shown what missing the Champions League costs, that is the appeal. A larger fixed fee, agreed early, is a part of revenue the league table does not decide.

The question Can Milan use its next kit deal to close the commercial gap with Juventus and Inter while it is outside the Champions League, or will any supplier price it on its recent results? Signing early locks in a fee for the 2030s; waiting lets the club negotiate after a better season but risks a weaker one. So the question is whether Milan’s brand is strong enough to command a Juventus-level fee now, or whether its kit price will follow its league position.
