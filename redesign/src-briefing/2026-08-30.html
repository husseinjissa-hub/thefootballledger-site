Football’s money keeps finding the side door.

Six stories from a week when football’s money kept going around the obstacles in front of it. UEFA gave up trying to out-vote Gianni Infantino and sued him in three American courts instead. Chelsea filled the Premier League’s most conspicuous empty shirt with a stablecoin. Newcastle spent £190m in the one place the spending rules do not look. DAZN stopped buying football rights and started buying the pipes they travel through. The Bundesliga handed live matches to an active free agent’s YouTube channel. And clubs across Europe went looking for revenue in their buildings rather than their teams.

Story 01

UEFA has stopped trying to out-vote Infantino. It is suing him instead.

Source · Front Office Sports / Off The Pitch · August 2026

On Thursday UEFA filed applications in three United States federal courts — in New York, Florida and Colorado — naming FIFA, Gianni Infantino, the investment firm Thrive Capital and the media executive Greg Maffei. The filings are requests for discovery, the American procedure that compels the other side to hand over documents. UEFA says it and unnamed others are preparing criminal complaints in Switzerland over how FIFA’s abandoned plan to sell part of its tournaments was structured, valued and marketed.

The allegation is what gives the case teeth. UEFA’s lawyers claim the proposed $4.2 billion sale of a 21% stake in FIFA Forward Enterprise — a new subsidiary that would have held the commercial rights to the men’s and women’s World Cups and the Club World Cup — was neither the product of an open, competitive auction nor tested by any independent valuer, and rested on what they call an “absurdly low enterprise value of only approximately $20 billion”. That shifts the argument from a governance quarrel to something nearer an allegation about the price itself, which is the predicate a Swiss criminal complaint would need.

Later the same day UEFA suspended, “on a provisional basis”, its threatened withdrawal of European teams from FIFA competitions, which looked like a retreat and was closer to a change of weapon. A boycott would have cost UEFA’s own members — European sides were about to miss an Under-20 Women’s World Cup. Discovery costs only the people on the receiving end. Meeting in Monaco, national-association representatives on UEFA’s executive committee, European FIFA Council members and European association presidents declared that trust in the office of FIFA president “has been broken and cannot be restored”, and said that should Infantino seek another mandate they would work with partner confederations to put up a credible alternative at the March 2027 election. Italy and Romania had withdrawn support the day before, six Nordic federations issued a joint demand, and Saudi Arabia’s federation backed Infantino only “at this time”.

The question UEFA is suing the organisation it belongs to, and has promised to run a candidate against its own president. Italy, Romania and six Nordic federations have already broken ranks. So the question is what FIFA’s authority over its members is worth after this — and whether the decisions that used to be settled in Zurich now get settled in coalitions and courtrooms instead.
Story 02

Chelsea sold the Premier League’s emptiest shirt to a stablecoin.

Source · CoinDesk / Sportcal · August 2026

Chelsea have filled the most conspicuous vacancy in English football. Circle, the American company behind the USDC stablecoin, takes the front of the men’s, women’s and academy shirts from Sunday — the first stablecoin brand on the front of a Premier League shirt. Terms were not disclosed and it is reported as an initial one-year deal with an option to extend; the club had been targeting a reported £50m–£65m a year. It ends three seasons of stopgap sponsors.

What made the category sellable was a licence. Circle’s UK arm holds an FCA electronic-money licence — not a stablecoin authorisation, since the UK regime for those does not begin until 2027 — and that licence is what let a crypto brand walk into the slot the gambling ban was meant to clean up. Which is the uncomfortable reading: the ban did not take regulated-risk money off the shirt. It changed which regulator signs it off — from the Gambling Commission to the FCA.

The same day supplied two more examples. Tottenham named VSbet its official regional betting partner for Asia; Leeds put the trading platform eToro across the LED boards at Elland Road. Neither touches the front of a shirt, which is all the ban covers. Nottingham Forest had already moved Bally’s from their shirt front to their sleeve a week after losing the front. The money is not leaving English football. It is relocating to the parts of the kit and the stadium the rule does not reach.

The question The ban was meant to take risk off the front of Premier League shirts. In its first season, the league’s most conspicuous empty shirt has gone to a stablecoin, and the bookmakers have moved to sleeves, hoardings and Asia-only deals. So the question is whether the rule reduced the risk, or simply moved it somewhere harder to see.
Story 03

Newcastle just spent £190m where the spending rules cannot see it.

Source · Insider Sport / Borussia Dortmund · August 2026

Newcastle have agreed to buy the 260-acre Woolsington Hall estate near Newcastle Airport and turn it into a first-team and academy campus in a scheme the club puts at £190 million, with a planning application due early in 2027 and occupation targeted for 2029-30. It is one of the largest single training-ground commitments English football has seen.

The interesting part is where that money sits in the accounts. Football’s spending rules police the income statement — wages, transfer fees written down year by year, the running cost of a squad. From this season, squad-cost ratios have replaced the profit and sustainability rules and tighten that further. Land and buildings are capital spending. They do not count towards any of it.

Set that against Dortmund in the same week. A well-run listed club whose advertising and sponsorship income actually rose posted a €21.7 million loss and pulled its dividend, mainly because it failed to reach the last 16 of the Champions League — going out in the knockout play-off to Atalanta — having reached the quarter-finals a year earlier. Regulation has spent five years learning to police the line clubs control least — how far they go in a knockout competition — while leaving alone the one that most durably separates the funded from the unfunded.

The question Squad-cost rules exist to stop wealthy owners buying an advantage through the wage bill. Nothing stops them buying one through land, buildings and facilities the rules do not count. So the question is whether football has limited what owners spend, or merely told them where to put it.
Story 04

DAZN has stopped buying football rights and started buying the pipes.

Source · PR Newswire / Hollywood Reporter · August 2026

DAZN is buying EverPass Media, the company holding exclusive rights to show NFL Sunday Ticket in American bars, restaurants and other commercial venues. It will be rebranded DAZN for Business. The unusual part is the payment: terms were not disclosed, but part of the consideration is DAZN’s own equity, so RedBird Capital, the NFL’s investment arm 32 Equity and the wrestling and boxing group TKO all become minority shareholders in DAZN.

A football-first streamer has therefore just acquired American strategic shareholders and a US venue network it could not have built on its own. And it has done so while its European rights portfolio shrinks — Ligue 1 abandoned mid-contract, Serie A’s UK rights renewed for a single season, one Premier League and one Champions League match per round in Portugal handed to a partner’s free YouTube channel.

Read together the direction is clear enough. DAZN is moving from buying the rights to owning the pipes those rights travel down, and out-of-home venues are one distribution layer an incumbent can defend against Amazon, Apple and Netflix. It also means the tender the French league is preparing for the autumn — for a 2029-34 cycle, and, if it lands, the fifth distinct French rights structure since Mediapro collapsed in 2020 — is being readied without the buyer that was until recently its most motivated bidder.

The question For a decade the assumption was that whoever paid most for football rights would control how football reaches people. DAZN has just spent equity on a venue network instead of cash on a league. So the question is whether owning the pipes is now worth more than owning what flows through them.
Story 05

The Bundesliga gave live matches to a YouTube channel.

Source · Insider Sport · August 2026

Seven Friday-night Bundesliga matches will be shown live and free in the UK and Ireland this season on “Jamie Vardy’s Having a Party”, the YouTube channel of the former Leicester and Cremonese striker, who is 39 and still looking for a club. The channel is about two months old and has roughly 14,400 subscribers. The run opened with Bayern against Stuttgart on 28 August. A second creator, Mark Goldbridge, was renewed for fifteen watch-alongs; his channel has 1.5 million subscribers, and 54% of his Bundesliga live audience last season was between 13 and 34.

The league now splits its UK distribution across Sky, Amazon, the BBC, Samsung TV Plus and two YouTube channels. That is the reverse of how the Premier League sells. English football’s overseas rights are worth roughly £2.1 billion a season because scarcity is manufactured and then defended — exclusive windows each locked to a single broadcaster in a territory, a protected Saturday blackout.

There is a trade underneath the strategy. A package nobody holds exclusively is a package nobody pays a premium for. Selling reach is the rational move when an audience will not follow you behind a paywall; selling exclusivity works when it will. The Bundesliga is choosing reach in a market where it has never converted exposure into subscriptions, which is either a considered bet on a younger audience or an admission about the price it can command.

The question The Bundesliga’s stated aim is reach, and a younger audience — 54% of one creator’s live viewers last season were aged 13 to 34. Giving matches away will certainly produce more viewers. So the question is whether reach is the objective in itself, or whether it only counts once those viewers become something the league can charge for.
Story 06

Clubs are chasing a revenue line that has nothing to do with football.

Source · TheStadiumBusiness / Portugoal · August 2026

Everton have asked Liverpool council for permission to double the number of full-capacity non-football events they may hold at the Hill Dickinson Stadium, from four a year to eight. No concert has been staged there yet. The 52,769-seat ground was underwritten in part on revenue the current planning permission caps.

They are not alone. A German-led consortium has paid a reported €40 million for 90% of the SAD, the corporate vehicle of the Portuguese club Estrela da Amadora — reported by Lusa as the largest sale of a controlling stake in a Portuguese football SAD on record — and the buyers’ own statement singled out not the squad but the fact that the club owns its own stadium. Cádiz will ask shareholders on or around 30 September to approve a reverse partial spin-off detaching its parent company, Sport City Cádiz — the vehicle holding the group’s planned arena and its Sportech City development — from the football club’s accounts.

The common thread is a revenue line that does not move with the league table. Matchday and broadcast income rise and fall with results; a building that hosts concerts, conferences and events does not. For clubs outside the handful that can count on European prize money, the ground is becoming the more predictable asset — and, in at least one case this week, the reason to buy the club at all. The Ledger set out how far that logic reaches in The 365-day stadium is a moat, not an evolution.

The question A football club’s income has always risen and fallen with how the team plays. Buildings are indifferent to results. So the question is what a club becomes when the most dependable part of its business is the part that has nothing to do with football.
