Story 01

Barcelona passed a billion with its stadium still half-open.

Source · FC Barcelona / Goal / SI · September 2026

Barcelona’s board has announced revenue above €1bn for the 2025-26 season, the first time the club has passed that mark. The club attributes the rise essentially to stadium income, which is the striking part, because for much of the season it was not in its stadium: matches were played at the Estadi Johan Cruyff and the Olympic stadium before a return to a Camp Nou capped at 42,000. Sponsorship income grew and the club’s retail subsidiary reported record merchandising.

The supporting lines move the same way. Wages absorb 54% of ordinary income, inside UEFA’s limit. Debt has fallen by around €90m to €469m. The club reports positive ordinary results for a third consecutive season. The margin behind that phrase is the number worth watching, and it arrives with the full audited accounts at the Ordinary General Assembly on 19 September — the clearest test yet of whether the recovery is still building.

What gives the stadium number its weight is what Barcelona has already committed elsewhere. As this Briefing reported in July, the club sold Sixth Street a quarter of its LaLiga television income for twenty-five years. With part of the media line spoken for that far ahead, growth has to come from what the club still owns outright — the ground, the sponsors and the shop. That is where this record came from, and it was earned at a fraction of the capacity to come: the reopening runs at around 62,000, with 105,000 planned by 2028.

The question Barcelona reached this record by growing what it still owns outright, after years of raising cash by selling future income. So the question is which instinct takes over the next time the club needs money — and whether the margin in the 19 September accounts shows a recovery that is compounding or one that is merely holding.
Story 02

AZ Alkmaar found a way to earn from its academy without owning anything.

Source · Off The Pitch · September 2026

AZ Alkmaar signed three partnerships in seventeen days in July — with Nancy in France, Zbrojovka Brno in the Czech Republic and Almere City at home. In each one the Dutch club sells the way it develops players, and is paid a fee plus a share of what those players are eventually sold for.

The difference from the usual arrangement is what changes hands. When a big club wants a smaller club’s production line, it buys the smaller club — and with it the players, the wage bill and the losses. AZ buys none of that. It sells the method, keeps a slice of the outcome, and carries no ownership of the club, the squad or the risk.

The record is what makes it sellable. AZ reports more than €85m generated from academy products since 2021-22, and eight homegrown players started each of its first five matches this season. Teaching that costs the club almost nothing it was not already doing.

The question AZ has found a way to sell what its academy knows without selling any part of itself. So the question is whether expertise becomes a real third revenue line for mid-sized clubs — or whether the ones with something worth teaching end up training their own future competitors.
Story 03

English clubs spent a record £3.4bn. A growing share went to each other.

Source · Inside World Football / Calcio e Finanza · September 2026

The Premier League’s summer window passed £3.41bn, beating last year’s £3.14bn and three times the £1.13bn of five years ago — and that count was still rising as deadline-day business settled. Enzo Fernández’s £125m move from Chelsea to Manchester City, confirmed just after the window shut, equalled the British record.

The number underneath is the one worth keeping. Deals between Premier League clubs made up 38% of fee transfers, up from 30% a year earlier, and 48% once purchases from the rest of the English pyramid are counted. England still spent more across borders than any country — $3.02bn of a $9.89bn global total, on FIFA’s count — but a growing share of the record is being paid to other English clubs.

That changes what the figure means. Money paid to a foreign club leaves the league and strengthens a rival market. Money paid to another Premier League club does not: it stays inside a group that already shares the same broadcast income, and it raises prices for everyone in it. The league gets richer relative to the leagues it used to buy from, and those leagues receive less of the money that kept them competitive.

The question If a rising share of English football’s record spending is paid to other English clubs, the money is not leaving to strengthen anyone else. So the question is whether the Premier League is buying talent or compounding a lead — and what a selling league does when the largest buyer increasingly shops at home.
Story 04

UEFA has run out of things to sell, so it sold the referee.

Source · Sportcal / Calcio e Finanza · September 2026

The workwear brand Strauss has become the first official partner of UEFA Refereeing, through 2028-29. Its logo goes on match officials’ sleeves from this week’s Champions League opener, spreads to the backs of their training and stadium jackets from 2027-28, and covers Euro 2028, the Women’s Euro in 2029, the Women’s Champions League and the full club programme. Nobody had ever sold the match official before.

It was announced on the same day as a Hisense renewal that is really a reduction. After a decade across all of UEFA’s men’s national-team competitions on a deal reported at more than $31m a year, Hisense is now a partner of Euro 2028 alone. New inventory was created on the day existing inventory shrank.

The pattern held all week. Haier bought exclusive worldwide home-appliance, television and commercial-display rights to the Champions League from 2027 to 2031 — another category the competition had never sold. Fanatics extended its trading-card deal to 2032-33. China’s iQIYI moved onto a four-year term at about $10m a season. UEFA’s commercial cycle is reportedly heading past €1bn in average annual value, and it is getting there by making the property smaller and selling more of them.

The question A rights-holder that keeps finding new things to sell looks like one in good health. A rights-holder whose biggest sponsor renews for one tournament instead of all of them does not. So the question is whether UEFA is growing the market or subdividing it because the large units will no longer hold their price.
Story 05

The women’s game just got its first clean price.

Source · Inside World Football · September 2026

London City Lionesses have signed a front-of-shirt deal with Nike that owner Michele Kang says is the largest women-only front-of-shirt deal in the game, beating the reported ~$4m at the NWSL expansion side Atlanta and worth more, she says, than some Premier League men’s shirts. Terms were not disclosed.

What makes it useful is that London City is the only WSL club that does not share a shirt sponsor with a men’s team. Every other number in the English women’s game is bundled: a sponsor buys both teams at once, and nobody outside the club knows how the value was split. This is the first time a women’s club has been priced entirely on its own.

That matters beyond one club. Until now a WSL side negotiating its own sponsorship had no public figure to argue from, only its share of somebody else’s deal. London City has created one. The next club to come to market will be the first able to point at what a women’s team is worth by itself.

The question One club with no men’s team attached has just proved what a women’s side is worth on its own. So the question is whether that number opens the category for the rest of the league — and whether a public benchmark drives the next round of women’s sponsorship deals higher, or hands buyers a figure to negotiate down to.
