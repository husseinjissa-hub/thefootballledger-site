Story 01

The Premier League’s offer to the EFL is reported at £1.8bn. Most Championship clubs would still fail the EFL’s new spending rule.

Source · Insider Sport, citing the Daily Mail / Leonard Curtis Football Finance Report · September 2026

The biggest money argument in English football is how much the Premier League shares with the 72 clubs of the EFL below it. The two leagues have been trying to agree a “new deal for football” since 2023. A package first valued at about £915m stalled that December and collapsed in March 2024, when Premier League clubs would not back it. Since then the Football Governance Act has created an Independent Football Regulator, and either league can now trigger its “backstop” powers, under which the regulator can impose a settlement if the leagues cannot agree themselves.

The Premier League has now made its biggest offer yet. Its clubs agreed a ten-year package unanimously in the summer and formally put it to the EFL in August. This month the Daily Mail reported it to be worth about £1.8bn in additional money, equivalent to about 17.5% of Premier League broadcast revenue against about 11% today. The EFL had asked for 25%. A mid-table Championship club would reportedly receive about £20.6m a season in total, up from about £12.1m. Neither league has published a breakdown. The offer comes with conditions:

  • The Carabao Cup semi-finals would become single matches, and the Premier League’s levy on transfer fees would rise from 4% to 6%.
  • Fifteen per cent of the money would have to be spent on club infrastructure.
  • A £20m fund would support clubs that fall into administration.
  • New limits would be placed on wage growth, alongside changes to how academy players are developed.

Insider Sport reports that the wage and academy proposals are designed to reduce clubs’ dependence on their owners’ money. The two sides are reported to be about £27m apart. The EFL is in no hurry. The regulator is reported to be publishing a draft of its first State of the Game report on 12 October, and a critical verdict on the pyramid’s finances would strengthen the EFL’s hand.

A report published on 16 September shows what the money would meet. Leonard Curtis, the insolvency firm, puts Championship revenue in 2024-25 at £920.3m. The four biggest earners were all clubs receiving parachute payments, the money the Premier League pays relegated clubs for up to three seasons to soften the drop. Together they generated £363.9m, almost 40% of the total. Over the past ten seasons, parachute clubs averaged 2.8 times the revenue of the rest and were more than four times as likely to be promoted.

The report then tests the EFL’s new Squad Cost Rule, in force from this season. It limits what a Championship club spends on players and its manager, transfer fees included, to 85% of its income. Applied to the 2024-25 accounts, 18 of 23 reporting clubs would have breached it, and 11 had squad costs above their entire income. The rule also lets owners top up spending with their own money. Once the report allows an owner top-up of £16m for the season, only three clubs still fail: Burnley, Leeds and Cardiff. The EFL’s approved rules cap top-ups at £15m a season and £33m over three years.

That last step is why the conditions matter as much as the cheque. On the report’s figures, most clubs that fail the rule pass it because an owner puts money in, not because the club earns more. More money from the Premier League raises what every club may spend. The conditions then decide how it is used: some of it locked into grounds and training facilities, wage growth held back, and a fund to catch clubs that fail. Whether that is protection or interference is the argument the EFL’s clubs are now having.

The question Would the EFL be better off taking the money with these conditions attached, or holding out for a larger sum with fewer strings? The conditions trade freedom for stability: less room to chase promotion on wages, more money forced into grounds and academies. So the question is whether they build a stronger EFL over ten years, one less dependent on owners’ money, or simply a more closely supervised one that stays as far behind the Premier League as it is today.
Story 02

Manchester United set a revenue record without playing in Europe. Celtic lost more than half its media income by dropping one competition.

Source · Manchester United plc / Celtic plc annual results · 22–23 September 2026

Most football clubs publish accounts once a year, months late, with little detail. A handful have shares that trade publicly and must report to investors on a timetable. Manchester United, listed in New York, and Celtic are two of them, and they reported a day apart this week: Celtic on 22 September, United on 23 September. Together they show what a European place is worth to a club’s accounts.

Celtic are the Scottish champions and played in the Champions League in 2024-25, reaching the knockout phase play-offs. In August 2025 they lost a qualifying play-off to Kairat Almaty of Kazakhstan on penalties and dropped into the Europa League. The accounts for the year to June 2026 show the cost:

  • Revenue fell 22.7%, from £143.6m to £111.0m.
  • Media rights income fell from £45.2m to £20.6m. The club says the drop also hit ticketing and retail.
  • Operating costs rose 4.3% to £122.1m.
  • A £33.9m profit after tax became a £4.8m loss.

Celtic still ended the year with £66.4m in cash, so this is a bad year, not a crisis. But most of the fall traces back to one penalty shoot-out in Almaty.

United’s year ran the other way. In 2024-25 they finished fifteenth in the Premier League but reached the Europa League final. Last season they had no European football at all and finished third. Revenue still reached a record £677.6m. Broadcasting rose 19.6% to £206.8m, because Premier League prize and television money rises with league position; commercial income fell 4.8% and matchday income 4.2%. Operating profit was £22.6m, against a loss of £18.4m, which the club puts down to cost and job cuts made earlier and to the better league finish. Below that line the picture is weaker. Net finance costs rose from £21.2m to £69.6m, largely because a currency gain on United’s dollar debt the year before became a loss, and the club lost £43.0m.

United’s guidance for this season, with the Champions League back at Old Trafford, is revenue of £740m to £760m. The club does not break that down, but Europe is likely to be a large part of the increase.

The two are not like for like, and that is the point. United earns six times what Celtic does, in a league whose television money dwarfs Scotland’s, and its commercial income alone, £317.3m, is almost three times Celtic’s entire revenue. That base let United set a record in a year without Europe. Celtic has no such cushion: when the Champions League money went, most of the gap between a profit and a loss went with it.

The question Is Champions League money becoming less of a financial prize for clubs with global brands and commercial strength, and more of a necessity for everyone else? United still expects Europe to lift its revenue this season, so the prize has not disappeared. But if the biggest clubs can set records without it while clubs like Celtic cannot balance their books without it, the competition widens the gap it rewards.
Story 03

Manchester City have reportedly been found in breach on 114 of 115 charges. There is no sanction yet. Rival clubs are already asking about compensation.

Source · The Athletic / BBC Sport / AP / ESPN · September 2026

Manchester City were bought in 2008 by Sheikh Mansour of Abu Dhabi and have won eight Premier League titles since. In February 2023 the Premier League charged them with 115 breaches of its financial rules. The central allegation was that City’s accounts from 2009-10 to 2017-18 did not give a true and fair view of their revenue, including sponsorship, or of their costs. Other charges concern the pay of a manager and players, UEFA’s rules, and City’s cooperation with the investigation. City have always denied wrongdoing. An earlier UEFA case over some of the same years ended in July 2020 when the Court of Arbitration for Sport lifted a two-year European ban, finding most of the alleged breaches either not established or time-barred.

The Premier League case was heard in London by a three-person independent commission between September and December 2024. On 25 September The Athletic reported, and BBC Sport and others followed, that the commission has found City in breach on 114 of the 115 charges. The decision has not been published. The Premier League has declined to comment, and it is not yet known which charge was not upheld or how the commission reasoned. City say the process “remains ongoing with significant elements to be completed”. No sanction has been set, and BBC Sport was told City will appeal if the finding goes against them.

A sanction is the sporting consequence. The financial one may come from other clubs. Several Premier League clubs are reported to be taking legal advice on whether they can claim compensation for prize money and European income they say they lost. Reports differ on whether they would claim one by one or through the league.

There is a recent precedent. Everton finished four points above the relegation places in 2021-22, and Burnley went down. Everton were later found to have broken the profit and sustainability rules for that period. Burnley argued that if the breach had been punished that season, it would have stayed up. In June an independent commission agreed and ordered Everton to pay Burnley £26m, plus £9.1m in interest. Everton are appealing.

The precedent now exists. A club that breaks the financial rules can be made to pay a rival what the breach cost it, and a commission will put a number on it. Unless Everton’s appeal overturns it, that principle stands. Burnley’s claim covered one relegation in one season and came to £35m with interest. The central allegations against City cover nine seasons, from 2009-10 to 2017-18, in which titles, European places and relegations were all decided. Each rival would still have to prove what it would have earned had City’s accounts been different, which is harder across nine seasons than across one. But the potential bill is of a different order.

The question If the finding survives appeal, the sanction will be the headline. The bigger bill may be the claims. So the question is whether rival clubs can turn nine seasons of disputed accounts into a number a commission will accept, and whether, after Everton, compensation becomes a standard consequence of breaking English football’s financial rules.
Story 04

Barcelona sold part of its future television money to investors. Now it is selling its best seats to fans, for up to thirty years.

Source · FC Barcelona / Legends Global / TheStadiumBusiness · September 2026

Personal seat licences are common in American football stadiums. A fan pays a large sum once for the right to a particular seat for many years, and the club has that money up front, often to help pay for the stadium. The model is new to European football.

With Legends Global, its hospitality partner, Barcelona has launched licences for about 2,000 VIP seats remaining at the redeveloped Camp Nou. A buyer makes an initial payment for exclusive use of one seat for 15 or 30 years, with hospitality attached. The club expects to sell them within about two and a half seasons and projects about €700m of committed revenue over the life of the licences. That is contracted income spread across up to three decades, not money in hand. The first phase of the programme, running since 2024, has signed nearly 5,000 seats for more than €380m of committed revenue, and all the suites and boxes are sold.

Barcelona has made this kind of trade before. In 2022, short of money and unable to register new players under LaLiga’s spending limits, it pulled what it called “levers”. It sold a quarter of its LaLiga television income for 25 years to the US fund Sixth Street, and 49% of Barça Studios, its audiovisual production arm, to other investors. The Ledger set out how that worked here. The Briefing has followed the consequences since:

  • In early September: revenue passed €1bn, and with part of the television money already sold, growth had to come from what the club still owned outright, meaning the ground, the sponsors and the shop.
  • On 19 September: members approved accounts showing record revenue of €1.06bn but net equity of minus €168m, and another €510m of borrowing, €210m of it secured on television rights. On figures the club presented to members, total financial debt is about €2.68bn, roughly €1.8bn of it tied to the stadium.

The seat licences are a version of the same move, applied to the ground. Income that would otherwise arrive season by season is committed now. The difference is the buyer, the club’s wealthiest supporters rather than a fund, and the asset, the premium seating the new stadium was built to earn from.

The question Barcelona has now sold part of its television income and part of its best seats in advance. Over the life of the stadium debt, how much of the new Camp Nou’s income will still be the club’s to collect? And will other European clubs building new grounds copy a model that brings the money forward?
Story 05

DAZN is adding competitions in its oldest markets, not opening new ones.

Source · Sportcal / Inside World Football / SportsPro · September 2026

A sports streaming service can grow in two ways. It can go into new countries, or it can add more to watch in the countries where it already has subscribers. DAZN has tried both. It launched in 2016 in Germany, Austria, Switzerland and Japan, then in 2020 opened in more than 200 markets. Its latest deals are the second kind. In the three German-speaking countries it has added rights from two football confederations:

  • From Africa’s confederation, CAF, according to reports this week: the 2027 Africa Cup of Nations and the 2026-27 CAF Champions League.
  • From UEFA, agreed in April: the main Champions League rights in Austria, and the Europa League and Conference League in Switzerland, from 2027-28 to 2030-31.

No fees have been disclosed, and the African deal has been reported rather than announced. The rights would replace Sportdigital, a smaller German channel that has shown the Cup of Nations there since 2021, although its coverage was already available on DAZN through a carriage deal. The timing helps: the 2027 tournament runs from 19 June to 17 July, when Europe’s domestic leagues are off.

The contrast is with France. In 2024 DAZN agreed to pay about €400m a year for most of Ligue 1’s matches, a bet on entering a big new market with a flagship league. It won an estimated 500,000 subscribers against the 1.5 million it reportedly needed to break even, and left after one season, paying the league €100m to end a five-year contract. DAZN made a pre-tax loss of $936m in 2024 and its chief executive told the Financial Times last October that the group would be profitable in 2026.

Set against that, these deals look like the other strategy. DAZN is not outbidding anyone for a flagship league or opening a new country. It is adding competitions in markets it has served for a decade, competitions that, though the fees are undisclosed, almost certainly cost far less than a top domestic league. At the end of August the Briefing described a related move in the United States, where DAZN agreed to buy EverPass, the business that shows NFL games in bars, in a deal that makes its sellers minority shareholders in DAZN. The common thread is depth in places it already knows, not breadth.

The question Can a deeper offer in the markets DAZN already knows do what the expensive push into France did not, and turn it into a lasting, profitable business? Or does a streamer without a flagship domestic league in its biggest markets stay dependent on extras like these, which add hours to a subscription without giving anyone a new reason to buy one?
Story 06

AB InBev takes UEFA’s beer rights from Heineken, at a reported €200m a year.

Source · Inside World Football / VRT / Sportcal / UEFA · September 2026

UEFA sells sponsorship of the Champions League, Europa League and Conference League by category: one bank, one airline, one beer. Each category is exclusive, so a brand that wins it locks its rivals out of Europe’s biggest club competition for the length of the deal. Heineken has held the beer category for about three decades.

From 2027-28 it will be Stella Artois, owned by AB InBev, the world’s largest brewer. The deal runs for six seasons, to 2032-33. Neither UEFA nor AB InBev has disclosed the fee. It has been reported at about €200m a year, against a reported €120m a year for Heineken’s current deal, which would make it about two-thirds more for the same category. AB InBev is reported to have secured the rights in 2025.

The timing matches a wider reset. The same six seasons are the first of a new commercial cycle for UEFA’s club competitions. The rights are now controlled by UC3, a joint venture between UEFA and European Football Clubs, the body formerly called the European Club Association, and from 2027 they will be sold by Relevent, the American company backed by the Miami Dolphins owner Stephen Ross. It replaces TEAM Marketing, which has sold them since the modern Champions League was created in the early 1990s.

What UEFA sold is not new: the same competitions, the same category, the same kind of rights. What changed is the contest for them. Beer is one of the few categories with two global companies big enough to want these rights exclusively, and only one of them held them. The reported figures are not confirmed, and a six-year deal may include rights or activation the old one did not. But on its face, the increase is what happens when the holder and a challenger both want the same thing.

Where the extra money goes matters as much as the price. Sponsorship fees go into one pool with the television money for UEFA’s club competitions. Under the current rules, for 2024-27, that pool is expected to be about €4.4bn a season, and it is split like this:

  • 10%, about €440m, goes in solidarity payments: €308m to clubs that do not play in the competitions and €132m to clubs knocked out in the qualifying rounds.
  • After costs, the clubs taking part receive 93.5% of what is left, about €3.3bn, as prize money. Almost three-quarters of that goes to Champions League clubs.
  • UEFA keeps the other 6.5% of that net amount.

On those rules, most of any increase would end up as prize money for the clubs already in the competitions, not in grounds, academies or the wider game. The split for the new cycle from 2027 is for UEFA and the clubs, through UC3, to set.

The question If the new cycle brings in more from sponsors like AB InBev, where does the money go? Under today’s rules most of it becomes prize money for the clubs already in the competitions, which widens the gap it pays for. So the question is whether UEFA and the clubs use the new cycle to send more of it into solidarity, infrastructure and development, or pay it out again as prize money to the same winners.
