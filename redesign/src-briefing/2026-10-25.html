Story 01

English men’s clubs paid agents £547.8m in a year. The regulator calls the fees “ridiculous”.

Source · The FA agent-fee report (1 April 2026) / The Esk · remarks reported 21 October 2026

When a club signs or sells a player, or renews a contract, an agent usually negotiates the deal. The agent is then paid a fee. The FA, English football’s governing body, collects what clubs pay and publishes it once a year. The latest figures came out on 1 April and cover 4 February 2025 to 2 February 2026.

They are back in the news because of what the regulator said. David Kogan, chair of the Independent Football Regulator, called agents’ fees “ridiculous” in remarks reported this week. He said he would “much prefer to see it being spent on players”. He also said the regulator does not support a salary cap and has no power to impose one. The FA’s figures show:

  • Men’s clubs paid £547.8m in total across the Premier League, the EFL and the National League System.
  • Premier League clubs paid £460.3m of that, up about 12.5% from £409.1m a year earlier.
  • Championship clubs paid £69.7m, League One £14.0m and League Two £2.6m.
  • Chelsea paid the most at £65.1m, then Aston Villa (£38.4m) and Manchester City (£37.4m). Newcastle were tenth at £20.3m.

The Premier League total is now about 75% above its 2019-20 level of £263.4m, on the FA figures as compiled by The Esk. It rose 29% in 2023-24, was flat in 2024-25 and rose again this year.

Last week’s Briefing reported the regulator’s finding that 94% of clubs in the top five divisions made a pre-tax loss in 2025/26, and that clubs paid £455m in interest in 2024/25. Agent fees are a second outflow of similar size. The periods and the clubs covered differ, so the two figures cannot be added or compared exactly. They show scale.

The distinction is between money paid for a deal and money paid to a player. A fee goes to an agent when a deal is done. A wage goes to the player for playing. Kogan said he would rather see the money go to players. The FA data does not show why fees rose, and it does not show how agents divide their income afterwards. It shows only what clubs paid. The reports of his remarks do not say whether the regulator plans any action on fees.

The question If agent fees are regulated at some point, how much does an agent need to earn from a deal? Premier League fees are up about 75% since 2019-20, the regulator calls them ridiculous and says it cannot cap wages, and the reports do not say what level it would regard as fair. So the question is whether regulation eventually sets what an agent can earn from a transfer, or whether fees keep rising with the transfer market.
Story 02

Valencia sells 42% at a €1.25bn valuation that includes debt. The price of the stake has not been disclosed.

Source · Reuters via Global Banking & Finance and Euronext / StadiumDB · 23 October 2026

The €1.25bn is an enterprise value, not an equity value. Enterprise value is what the club is worth including its debt. Equity value is what the shares are worth once debt is counted out. Reuters describes the valuation as including debt, and the amount of that debt has not been disclosed. So the number cannot be turned into a price for the 42% stake.

On 23 October Valencia announced that Pitchwise Holdings will buy 42% of the club from Meriton Holdings, the company of Peter Lim. As reported by Reuters:

  • Buyer: Pitchwise, a group of family offices and long-term institutional investors led by sports executives Ann Rodriguez and Corey Woolfolk. Its backers include KKR co-founders George Roberts and Henry Kravis, and Penner Sports Group, a Denver holding company whose holdings include the NFL’s Denver Broncos.
  • Control: Meriton keeps a majority and Kiat Lim stays president. Rodriguez and Woolfolk join the ownership group.
  • New money: investors will also put €100m into the club.
  • Conditions: closing needs regulatory approval. No closing date has been given.

Valencia said the valuation reflects its men’s and women’s teams, the 49,000-seat Mestalla and the Nou Mestalla project, a new stadium planned for more than 70,000 fans.

The new stadium is the unfinished part. In 2025 the club raised about €322m to complete it: a €237m bond with a 28-year term, sold to US investors, and an €85m five-year loan. Another €80m left from LaLiga’s deal with the investor CVC is also meant for the stadium. According to StadiumDB, the bond is to be repaid mainly by selling the current Mestalla site once Nou Mestalla is finished. Completion is scheduled for summer 2027. The same site is therefore named in the valuation and in the repayment plan.

Two things follow from the reported terms. The €100m goes into the club. The price of the 42% goes to Meriton, and it has not been disclosed. The distinction the story turns on is between buying a football club and buying a stadium project. The disclosed pieces are a valuation that includes debt, €100m of new capital and a stadium financed in 2025. The cash price for the stake is the missing number.

The question What does the €1.25bn actually include? Valencia says it reflects the men’s and women’s teams, the old Mestalla and the new stadium project, on top of debt that has not been disclosed, and the price of the 42% is unknown. So the question is whether the number prices a football club that happens to need a new ground, or a stadium project that comes with a football club.
Story 03

Two member-owned clubs are bringing in outside investors. Each keeps a legal floor on its control.

Source · Sky Sport Germany / Private Banking Magazin citing Manager Magazin / Yahoo Sports · October 2026

Two clubs owned by their members are bringing in outside shareholders this month, one done and one proposed. Each has a rule that sets how much control must stay with the members. The rule is the thing to watch.

FC Bayern München AG, Bayern’s football company, has been owned 75% by the members’ association, with Adidas, Audi and Allianz holding 8.33% each. The association’s statutes allow outsiders to hold at most 30%. This week Bayern confirmed that Viessmann Generations Group will take just under 6.7% through a capital increase, which means new shares are issued and the money goes to the club. The terms as reported:

  • Price: not disclosed by Bayern. Manager Magazin, a German business magazine, reports about €250m.
  • Implied value: €250m for just under 6.7% implies about €3.7bn for the company after the money goes in. That is Ledger arithmetic from a reported price.
  • Ownership after: the association falls from 75% to 70%, the statutory minimum. Adidas, Audi and Allianz fall to just under 7.8% each.
  • History: Allianz’s 2014 purchase of 8.33% for €110m implied about €1.3bn.

AFC Wimbledon is much smaller and sits at the other end. The League One club is controlled by the Dons Trust, its supporters’ trust, which holds 50.01% of the voting shares in the company that runs the club. A consortium called SSC, led by former England defender Ryan Bertrand, has proposed £87m: £16m on completion for 34% of the company, then £71m over five seasons. The Trust’s board recommends a yes vote, and members can vote until 17 November. The Trust would keep:

  • its 50.01% of the voting shares, with its voting rights unaffected;
  • a veto over the stadium freehold, and over the club’s name, badge, colours, ground and constitution;
  • first refusal if SSC sells, and the right to block investors it considers unsuitable.

SSC would take two of the club’s 12 board seats and an observer place. If it misses a payment, its share of the club’s value stops growing, which implies that its share rises as it pays. Inside World Football reports the structure could ultimately give SSC up to 90% of the club’s economic interest, and that members need a 75% majority to approve. If the £16m buys the 34% outright, it implies about £47m for the whole company once the money is in.

The distinction is between votes and value. Both clubs protect the votes. Bayern’s floor is 70% of the shares. Wimbledon’s is 50.01% of the voting shares, which does not fix how much of the club’s value outsiders can come to own. Bayern has now sold everything its statutes allow. Wimbledon’s members are being asked to accept a veto and a vote as protection, on terms where the investor’s share of value can change. Neither price shows what going without control is worth, because neither club has sold a controlling stake to compare it with.

The question How much is control worth in the price of a stake? Bayern’s outside investors hold shares under a floor that keeps 70% with the members, and Wimbledon’s investor would pay for 34% while the Trust keeps its votes, vetoes and first refusal. So the question is whether investors pay less per share because it comes without control, or pay a full price for the club’s name and growth and accept that control stays with the members.
Story 04

PIF is reportedly preparing to sell 70% of Al Nassr. It is first clearing the club’s debt through its own companies.

Source · Semafor / Inside World Football · 19–20 October 2026

The Public Investment Fund, or PIF, is Saudi Arabia’s sovereign wealth fund. It took control of four Saudi clubs in 2023: Al Nassr, Al Hilal, Al Ittihad and Al Ahli. It has since begun to move some of them to private owners. In September it completed the sale of 70% of Al Hilal to Kingdom Holding, the company of Prince Alwaleed bin Talal, for SAR840m, according to Inside World Football. That valued the club’s equity at SAR1.2bn.

Semafor reported on 19 October that Al Nassr, Cristiano Ronaldo’s club, may be next. The talks are early, and one person close to PIF told Semafor a deal might not happen. As reported:

  • Buyer: a group led by RedBird Capital, the owner of AC Milan, which has held at least two meetings with PIF. Saudi Media Company is named as a possible investor.
  • Price: about SAR1bn ($267m) for 70%. Inside World Football calculates an equity value of about SAR1.43bn ($381m), a straight proportion that ignores debt.
  • Ronaldo: expected to hold up to 20%. How his stake is funded has not been settled.

Before any sale, PIF is reportedly arranging sponsorship deals between Al Nassr and companies in its own portfolio, to pay off about SAR800m ($213m) of the club’s debt. The money would come from companies in PIF’s portfolio, go to a club PIF controls, and clear the debt before the club passes to a buyer. Al Nassr’s spending was already limited earlier this year, with future transfers tied to the commercial income it can generate rather than shareholder support, Inside World Football reports. RedBird and PIF declined to comment.

The distinction is between what a buyer pays and what it gets. The SAR800m of debt equals 80% of the SAR1bn reported price for 70%. On the reported terms the buyer receives a club with that debt already cleared, paid for by sponsors in PIF’s portfolio. The reports do not say what those sponsors are paying, or whether they would keep paying after the sale. This may be a normal clean-up before a sale. The reports do not settle it.

RedBird already owns AC Milan and Toulouse and holds a minority stake in Liverpool’s owner, Fenway Sports Group. Investors are reportedly interested in using Ronaldo’s profile to build Al Nassr in the US. The buyer would be a multi-club owner with a commercial plan, taking over a club whose finances were first tidied by its seller’s other companies. PIF took control of four clubs in 2023. One has been sold and a second is in talks. The reports reviewed do not say what happens to the other two.

The question Is about SAR1bn for 70% a fair price for a club PIF has controlled since 2023, and will other top Saudi clubs follow? Al Hilal sold at an implied equity value of SAR1.2bn, Al Nassr’s implied value is about SAR1.43bn before debt, and PIF is clearing the debt first. So the question is whether RedBird is the first of several private buyers who will pay for Saudi clubs once the state has cleaned them up, or a one-off buyer drawn by a club with Ronaldo’s profile.
Story 05

FIFA has opened bidding for its 2030 US World Cup rights. It has not said whether English and Spanish rights will be sold together.

Source · Inside World Football · 23 October 2026

FIFA sells the television rights to the men’s World Cup country by country. In the US, English and Spanish rights have been held by different companies. Fox has carried English-language coverage and Telemundo Spanish-language coverage of the last three men’s tournaments, according to Inside World Football.

This week FIFA opened a tender for the 2030 tournament, with 2034 as an option. The terms as reported:

  • Territory: the United States and Puerto Rico. Bids are due on 19 November.
  • Structure: FIFA allows more than one rights holder and has not said whether English and Spanish rights will be sold together.
  • Value: estimates put the two tournaments together at up to $4bn.
  • Contenders: earlier industry reporting named ESPN, Netflix, YouTube and Amazon Prime Video alongside Fox and Telemundo.

The Briefing of 4 October reported Netflix saying it will always be in the conversation for World Cup rights, and US bidders reportedly budgeting up to $2bn a tournament. The tender turns that into a process with a date.

The 2026 tournament in North America sets the baseline. It averaged 13.5 million US viewers across 104 matches, up 166% on Qatar 2022. Every US team match drew more than 20 million and the final averaged 63 million. The 2030 tournament is in Spain, Portugal and Morocco, with three centenary matches in Argentina, Paraguay and Uruguay, and 2034 is in Saudi Arabia. Inside World Football says both will likely have less convenient kick-off times for US viewers, and that US audiences are expected to fall.

The distinction is between selling one asset and selling several. A single package favours bidders who can pay for everything. Separate English and Spanish packages let more bidders in, but each is a smaller prize. The same logic applies to splitting by tournament or by round. Which approach raises more money is an argument, not a fact, and the tender allows either.

The audience figures come from a home World Cup, and the next two are not. Bidders will be pricing a tournament that is likely to be less convenient to watch than the one they have just seen. The reports do not say whether more rights holders would mean more viewers. The tender leaves FIFA free to choose how to cut the rights.

The question If FIFA splits the US rights, does it raise more in total and reach more viewers, or just add bidders who each pay less? One buyer able to pay for everything keeps the sale simple, and several buyers could compete for each part, but the audience behind the last price came from a tournament played at home. So the question is whether one large package for the few that can afford it, or smaller pieces that more bidders can reach, gets FIFA the most money and the biggest audience.
Story 06

Real Madrid wants six Premier League clubs to pay towards the Super League’s set-up costs. The estimated €80m comes from one source.

Source · Inside World Football · 21 October 2026

The European Super League was announced in April 2021 with 12 founding clubs. Nine withdrew within days: the six English clubs (Arsenal, Chelsea, Liverpool, Manchester City, Manchester United and Tottenham) plus Atlético Madrid, AC Milan and Inter. Juventus said in 2023 that it had left. Barcelona formally withdrew in February 2026. On those reports, Real Madrid is the only founder that has not withdrawn.

This week Inside World Football reported that Real Madrid wants the six English clubs to share the project’s set-up costs. The claim and the facts are separate:

  • The claim: Real Madrid believes the six clubs remain liable. One source puts the costs at up to an estimated €80m, a single-source total with no split between clubs.
  • The basis: all 12 founders signed a shareholders’ agreement for the European Super League Company, a Spanish holding company. Whether the English clubs owe anything is likely to depend on how its commitment to “jointly own and manage” the project is read, and whether that includes payments.
  • Who is owed: a Real Madrid source says any money would be owed to the Company.
  • Who has paid so far: Real Madrid, Barcelona and Juventus have covered the promotional costs to date.
  • The response: no response from the six clubs has been reported, and A22, the Super League’s promoter, declined to comment. No deadline has been reported.

After the nine exits in 2021, Real Madrid’s president Florentino Pérez said: “I’m not going to take my time to explain what a binding contract is here. But the fact is, the clubs can’t leave.”

The distinction is between leaving a competition and leaving a company. The six clubs withdrew from the Super League in 2021. Real Madrid’s argument, as reported, is about an agreement they signed to own a company. The reports do not say how a court would read that agreement, and they do not mention legal proceedings.

If the claim stands, part of the cost of the Super League falls on clubs that left it, and the three that stayed longest stop carrying it alone. If it does not, the cost stays with them. Split equally the estimated €80m would be about €13m a club, but no split has been reported. A22 has since proposed other formats, most recently the Unify League, a 96-club competition in four tiers.

The question Will the six clubs actually pay, and if not, what comes next? Real Madrid says they remain liable for an estimated €80m, a sum large enough to matter even split six ways, and no response from the clubs has been reported while A22 is already proposing a different competition. So the question is whether the clubs settle the bill, or whether the dispute ends in a new arrangement between the same clubs for a different competition.
