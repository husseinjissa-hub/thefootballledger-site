Story 01

Women players threaten legal action over World Cup prize money. The players’ union will not be the one to sue.

Source · Inside World Football · 30 October and 6 November 2026; The Athletic via OutKick; SportBusiness

A World Cup prize pot is paid by FIFA to the federations whose teams take part, and FIFA sets its size. Players are paid through their federation. The letter reported by The Athletic asks FIFA to maintain the minimum 30% share guaranteed to players.

The 2023 Women’s World Cup paid a record $110m in prize money, plus about $40m in preparation and club payments. The men’s tournament this summer had a prize pot of $655m. FIFA is now reported to be doubling the women’s pot to $220m for 2027, in Brazil. Inside World Football says FIFA has confirmed $2.5m in preparation money per team, matching the men’s teams this year.

The gap depends on how it is measured. The men’s $655m was shared by 48 teams, about $13.6m a team. The 2027 women’s tournament has 32 teams, so $220m is about $6.9m a team, roughly half. That is the distinction the story turns on. The players’ letter asks for “proportional equal prize money”, which on these numbers means a pot scaled to the number of teams, not the men’s total.

In September, FIFPRO, the global players’ union body, and representatives of 45 nations wrote to FIFA. They asked for proportional equal prize money and a binding collective agreement between FIFA and FIFPRO. The Athletic has since reported that FIFPRO and national unions have sought legal advice. Inside World Football says one unnamed national union is ready to go to court. The basis cited is Article 23 of FIFA’s Code of Ethics, which covers discrimination. No case has been reported as filed.

FIFPRO itself will not sue. In June it signed a cooperation agreement with FIFA and dropped a legal challenge over the transfer market, SportBusiness reported. FIFA and FIFPRO now say they are negotiating 2027 terms, including, for the first time, payments to associations and players who took part in qualifying. That leaves national unions, and a rule in FIFA’s own code.

FIFA’s case is that prize money is only part of what it spends. Its annual report, as reported in Brazil, puts total investment in the 2027 tournament at about $800m, nearly double 2023. The players’ side points to projected FIFA reserves of $6bn.

The letter asks for a binding agreement. Without one, the 2027 number is FIFA’s decision, and the body that would sign such an agreement has stepped back from court.

The question Who can make FIFA pay more now that FIFPRO has stepped back from litigation? Either the talks produce a binding agreement that fixes the women’s pool in writing, or 2027 is again a figure FIFA sets alone.
Story 02

Milan are reported to be returning to Adidas on about $56m a season. The money starts in 2028, after two more seasons.

Source · Inside World Football · 6 November 2026; Calcio e Finanza (citing Gazzetta dello Sport) · 4 November 2026; Milano Finanza

A kit deal is a contract under which a sportswear company pays a club every year for the right to make and sell its playing kit.

Gazzetta dello Sport, cited by Calcio e Finanza, reports that Milan will return to Adidas from summer 2028 on a ten-year deal worth €50m a season. Inside World Football puts it at about $56m. The reports carry no confirmation from Milan or Adidas. It would replace Puma, Milan’s supplier since 2018.

Puma’s deal started at around $15.7m a year and was renewed in 2022 at about $33.6m, Inside World Football says. The reported Adidas figure is about $22m a year more, roughly two-thirds. Milano Finanza says it would put Milan ahead of every other major Italian kit deal:

  • Milan with Adidas (reported): about $56m a season from 2028.
  • Juventus with Adidas: €408m fixed over ten seasons, about $46m a year before royalties.
  • Inter with Nike: €30m a year over eight years from 2023, about $34m.

Adidas already supplies Italy’s national team, Juventus and Roma, and is reported to be taking over as Serie A’s ball supplier from 2027/28. That suggests a wider push in Italian football. It is a reading of the contracts, not something Adidas has said.

Milan’s latest accounts give the scale. In 2025/26 the club lost about $27m on revenue of about $520m, after missing European football despite record sponsorship income, Inside World Football says. The extra $22m a year is more than four-fifths of that loss. The comparison joins a future contract to a past result, so it shows size rather than a plan.

The distinction the story turns on is between income that has been signed and income that has been received. A ten-year contract with a global brand is something a lender or a buyer can value today. It does not change Milan’s accounts until 2028, and Puma remains the supplier until then. Last week’s Briefing reported Milan and Inter’s naming deal with Generali for a stadium not due to open until 2031/32. Both look years ahead.

What RedBird, Milan’s owner, does with a contract of that size is not known. A club can treat it as future income. It can also use it to support borrowing today. Nothing in the reports says which Milan intend.

The question How and when will Milan use the extra $22m a year? It only starts in 2028, so the question is whether it makes the club more competitive in Italy and Europe, or mostly covers an operating gap like last season’s $27m loss.
Story 03

Porto are selling a $45m bond whose interest rate rises with Champions League places and league titles. The most it can add is 0.75 percentage points.

Source · Inside World Football · 2 November 2026; A Bola (interview with Porto’s chief financial officer) · 29 October 2026; Portuguese financial-education site summarising the prospectus; ECO

A club bond is a loan from many small investors instead of one bank. The club pays interest, the coupon, twice a year and repays the loan at the end. The new issue is a €40m bond, about $45m, maturing in December 2030. Subscriptions close on 13 November. It is open to investors resident in Portugal, from €2,500, about $2,800.

The twist is that the rate can rise with results:

  • A base rate of 5.25% a year, which Porto’s chief financial officer, José Pedro Pereira da Costa, says is guaranteed. It never falls.
  • A rise of 0.15 percentage points each time Porto qualify for the Champions League league phase.
  • A rise of 0.10 points each time Porto win the league.
  • A 6% ceiling, reached only with both in each of the three seasons from 2026/27 to 2028/29. Each rise is permanent once triggered.

Porto say it is the first football bond to link returns to results. That is the club’s claim.

The money at stake is small. The maximum rise is 0.75 points, about $0.3m a year on a $45m bond at the end of the term, against about $2.3m a year in base interest. If Porto have a poor season, the coupon does not fall. The club pays more only in years it has earned more.

The bond mainly refinances a €55m bond, about $62m, issued in 2023, which pays 6.25% and falls due on 7 December. Even at the ceiling the new bond would cost less. But €40m does not cover €55m, so Porto can raise the size, subject to approval, if they decide by 10 November.

The risk investors carry sits elsewhere. According to a summary of the prospectus on a Portuguese financial-education site, the bond is unsecured and unrated. The same summary lists:

  • Negative equity of about $184m in the parent company’s own accounts and about $33m on a consolidated basis.
  • An auditor’s note, from Ernst & Young, of material uncertainty about the company’s ability to continue as a going concern.
  • Player sales made up 39% of income in 2025/26. The Briefing of 18 October reported a 2025/26 loss of about $24m.
  • Further debts: about $24m due in November 2027, about $56m in December 2028, then the $129m Dragon Notes, raised in 2024 from US institutional investors, from November 2028.

The summary says the Dragon Notes sit in a subsidiary, Porto StadCo, and are paid first from its dividends. Only what is left reaches the parent company that issues this bond.

So the coupon is tied to results in a small way, and repayment in a large way. The summary also lists UEFA fines in 2024 and 2025 as a risk. The step-ups share some upside with investors. They do not change the risk of repayment.

The question Is a coupon that rises by at most 0.75 points, about $0.3m a year, a real sharing of risk or just a small sweetener? The risk that matters to investors is whether a club with negative equity can repay in 2030.
Story 04

Prediction markets are now a customer for football data. Sportradar’s rights bill rose 54% in a quarter in which its revenue rose 24%.

Source · Inside World Football · 5 November 2026; Sportradar third-quarter results release; iGaming Business · 8 June 2026; Gambling.com · 7 August 2026

A prediction market lets people trade on the outcome of an event, such as a match result. To pay out, it has to decide what happened, and it needs a source it can trust. That is called settling a contract. When Kalshi signed Sportradar in June, its chief executive said the company was using official league data “to ensure quicker trade settlements”.

Sportradar sells official sports data and integrity services, meaning tools that spot match-fixing and suspicious betting. It reports in euros; the figures here are Inside World Football’s dollar conversions. It has signed Robinhood, Kalshi, Polymarket and Novig, and its Polymarket deal covers more than 20 leagues, including MLS, La Liga and Serie A. Its third quarter, reported this week, showed:

  • Revenue of $405.6m, up 24%. US revenue rose 47% and is now 27% of the total, from 23%.
  • Betting and gaming content, the data side, grew 37%. The company credits IMG Arena and prediction-market customers together and does not split them.
  • Sports rights spending rose from $81.2m to $125m, up 54%. Over nine months rights expenses rose 31% and revenue 18%.
  • Adjusted EBITDA margin, the company’s measure of operating profit, slipped to 28.2% from 29.0%.

Rights spending is the cost of the licences that let Sportradar supply official data. Leagues and federations own the data, and a supplier that wants to be the official source has to pay for the right.

Sportradar does not hold these customers alone. In August Genius Sports announced it would supply official data to settle contracts on Kalshi and on Polymarket’s US platform. None of the companies has disclosed financial terms. Sportradar’s chief financial officer, Craig Felenstein, says its contracts mix fixed and variable fees and that he expects “significant growth for prediction markets next year”.

Year-to-date profit looks worse, $19.3m against $107.3m, but currency appears to explain much of it. Inside World Football reports a $27.8m currency loss this year against a $91.5m gain last year. That swing of about $119m is larger than the $88m fall. These are one outlet’s figures and ignore tax, so they show direction, not proof.

One more risk: Sportradar’s Brazilian business runs at about $67m a year, according to Inside World Football, and Brazil’s provisional betting ban, covered in last week’s Briefing, is due for debate in Congress this month.

The distinction the story turns on is between selling data and owning the right to supply it. Prediction markets are a new buyer, but the reports do not show who keeps the margin. Leagues can raise the price of the right, another supplier sells to the same markets, and Sportradar’s rights costs are rising faster than its sales.

The question Who captures the margin when prediction markets settle football contracts on official data? Either the leagues that own the data take it through higher rights fees, or suppliers like Sportradar keep it through scale.
Story 05

El Clásico set a US audience record for LaLiga. In China, state television suspended its games on 26 October. In China the dispute is not about ratings.

Source · Inside World Football · 4 August and 6 November 2026; Al Jazeera · 2 November 2026; The Sun (Malaysia)

A LaLiga source quoted by The Sun in Malaysia said China and the United States together finance the majority of its international broadcasting rights, valued at €748m, about $834m, for 2024/25. The report gives no split.

In the United States, ESPN holds the rights. The latest Clásico averaged 2.4m viewers across ABC and ESPN Deportes, which ESPN says is the most-watched LaLiga match in US history. Last season’s first Clásico drew 1.1m. These are ESPN’s figures.

Three things limit the comparison. The reports do not say where last season’s Clásico aired, so part of the rise may come from ABC, a broadcast network. Nielsen research cited by Inside World Football credits this summer’s World Cup with adding nearly 10m US soccer fans in a year. And the base is small: Premier League audiences on NBC and USA Network are more than three times LaLiga’s average.

China is a different kind of market. Migu, a streaming service owned by the state-owned China Mobile, signed in August to be LaLiga’s exclusive new-media partner in mainland China for four seasons, 2026/27 to 2029/30, covering all 380 matches a season. The value was not disclosed. LaLiga president Javier Tebas called China “a strategic market”.

On 26 October China Media Group, the state media body that oversees CCTV, suspended LaLiga broadcasts. The cause was an April video, which went viral last month, in which Kylian Mbappé joked about a meme placing his face on an image of Mao Zedong and called Mao a “Chinese dictator”. CMG said apologies from LaLiga, Mbappé and Real Madrid were an “absolute prerequisite”. All three have apologised. CMG has acknowledged them, but it is unclear whether talks have begun.

As of 2 November none of the matchweek 11 games played so far had been shown live on CCTV, and Real Madrid’s match did not appear to be on Migu either, though Barcelona’s was. Chinese sponsors have dropped Mbappé, with no values given. Real Madrid earn 10% to 15% of their shirt and merchandise sales in China, according to Marca.

The distinction the story turns on is between an audience and a permission. In the United States, growth depends on viewers choosing LaLiga. In China, the return of the broadcast depends on state-owned media that LaLiga does not control, and the stated condition was apologies, not audience. No fee has been disclosed for either market, so the reports cannot say which is worth more.

The question What does China’s blackout mean commercially for LaLiga? Will US growth offset it, or will clubs in Spain feel the loss?
