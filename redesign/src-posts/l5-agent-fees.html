Agent fees doubled. The agents' cut barely moved.

Premier League clubs paid agents £460.3m in 2025-26, and global agent fees have doubled since 2019. FIFA's data shows agents taking roughly the same tenth of transfer spending throughout. The problem is the size of the pie, not the slice, and that is what a fee cap would miss.

Premier League clubs paid agents £460.3m in 2025-26, a 75% increase on 2019-20. The figure has prompted criticism from regulators, but it raises a more interesting question: are agents taking a bigger cut, or is football simply generating more money for everyone involved?

How clubs pay agents

To understand how agents are paid, it is important to understand their relationships with clubs. Clubs pay agents when they sign a player, sell a player or renew a contract. Fees are usually a share of either the player's wages (for signings and renewals) or the transfer fee (for sales). When a club buys a player, the agent's fee is not based on the transfer fee paid to the old club; instead, it is calculated as a percentage of the player's new wages, because signing a new player involves signing a new employment contract.

FIFA's agent regulations place maximum fee caps on these commissions, with the exact limit depending on whom the agent represents. An agent can act on behalf of a single party, the player, the buying club or the selling club, or, in permitted cases, represent both the player and the buying club in the same deal. Under FIFA's fee caps, commissions for representing the player, the buying club, or both are capped as a share of the player's wages, while fees for representing the selling club are capped as a portion of the transfer fee.

Because national and international bodies track these transactions differently, the English and global agent fee figures in this article cannot be combined. The FA (The Football Association) records everything Premier League clubs pay agents across all domestic and international business, including contract renewals and new signings. FIFA, by contrast, only reports club-side agent fees tied to international transfers, so dividing one figure by the other would produce a meaningless ratio, which this analysis avoids.

The English numbers

English fees were flat at around £270m for three years, then climbed in three steps. First, clubs paid roughly £263.4m to £272.6m from 2019 to 2022. Covid did not push them down, and it did not push them up. Only in recent years have we seen a substantial increase in agent fees. They rose to £318.2m in 2022-23, a 17% increase. They jumped to £409.6m in 2023-24, up 29%. They held at £409.1m in 2024-25, then rose 12.5% to £460.3m in 2025-26. Chelsea paid the most in 2025-26 at £65.1m, ahead of Aston Villa at £38.4m and Manchester City at £37.4m.

The FA data shows what clubs paid. It does not show why. Fees are the result of three things: how many deals clubs do, how big those deals are, and what share agents take. A rise in the total could come from any of them. If clubs did more or bigger deals, agents could be paid more without taking a bigger cut. In recent years, there has been a clear inflation in transfer fees. The most expensive players ever bought by each club are Enzo Fernández for Manchester City, Alexander Isak for Liverpool, and Morgan Rogers for Chelsea. All three deals were completed during the 2025 and 2026 summer transfer windows. If agents raised their rate, the total would rise even with no change in the market. The FA's figures cannot separate higher commission rates from rising transfer values, because the FA only publishes a single lump-sum cash total per club. To test whether agents are actually taking a larger cut, we need a ratio of fees to transfer spend, which requires turning to FIFA's data.

The real test: the agents' cut

FIFA's data tracks international men's transfer spending and the fees clubs paid to agents on those deals. Unlike the FA's, these figures are reported in dollars.

Between 2019 and 2025, fees rose from $654.7m to $1.37bn, a rise of about 109%. Spending rose from $7.35bn to $13.08bn, a rise of 78%. The share tells the fuller story. It was 8.9% in 2019, and reached 10.5% in 2025.

Across all seven years, the agents' cut hovered tightly around 9%, with a spread of 2.2 percentage points between its lowest year (8.3%) and its highest (10.5%). While there has been no steady year-on-year climb, 2025 still reached a seven-year high. Looking strictly from 2019 to 2025, that share increased by 1.6 percentage points, a relative rise of about 18%.

Seven data points are a thin base; we cannot say whether 2025 marks a new level or one exceptional year. The record gives a hint. The share hit 10.3% in 2021, then dropped back the next year. A high year has been followed by a retreat; therefore, assuming that it will keep rising in 2026 and 2027 would be premature. What the rules try to do

The FIFA Football Agent Regulations (FFAR) were approved by FIFA in December 2022 and brought into force across two phases in 2023, with the core fee and representation rules taking effect on 1 October 2023. Their purpose was to overhaul a deregulated intermediary market. Under Article 15 of the FFAR, maximum service fee caps are dictated by two factors: who the agent represents and the annual salary of the individual involved.

An agent acting for a player, or for the club signing him, can take 3% of the player's pay, rising to 5% if the player earns $200,000 a year or less. An agent acting for both sides can take 6%, or 10% for lower earners. An agent acting for the selling club can take 10% of the transfer fee. Only that last cap is measured on the same basis as FIFA's spending data. The others are shares of wages, so they cannot be set directly against the 8.3% to 10.5% band; the comparison is useful but rough.

The caps never fully took hold. Courts in several countries suspended parts of the regulations, and England has operated without an active fee cap since the end of 2023. On 16 July 2026, however, the EU Court of Justice ruled that a cap on agents' fees can, in principle, be justified, leaving the German courts that referred the case to decide whether FIFA's rules meet that test. FIFA is now pushing to reach a negotiated agreement with agents before its revamped transfer system launches on 1 January 2027. The 10% cap on selling-club agents sits right inside the market average. If a cap matches what agents already take, it is fair to ask what it changes in reality.

The contrarian read

According to the data, then, can we conclude agents are not the problem? Over a seven-year span, agents have consistently taken roughly a tenth (between 8.3% and 10.5%) of worldwide transfer spending. Viewed through that lens, agents don't seem to have gotten greedier but have only been part of the overall market growth.

Yet the underlying cash totals tell a sharper story. Between 2019-20 and 2025-26, English clubs' payments to agents climbed 75% to £460.3m, while global club-side agent fees surged 109% over the comparable period (from 2019 to 2025) to reach $1.37bn. Rather than showing restraint, both figures point to an unchecked market where the sheer volume of money leaving the game, not just the percentage rate, is the core issue.

A temporary cooldown in 2024, when global agent fees eased to $710m, briefly suggested that spending growth might be levelling off. Instead, 2025 shattered records: with FIFA's fee caps still suspended, worldwide commissions jumped by over 90% to $1.37bn. In an unregulated market, agents do not merely drift alongside normal growth; they cash in dramatically during spending booms. A "roughly 10%" share sounds modest until it equals $1.37bn.

The contrarian takeaway is that percentage caps alone may miss the real driver of agent wealth: agents have only slightly expanded their slice of the pie, while the pie itself has grown massively.

That conclusion still comes with an important caveat. Because FIFA only tracks club-side fees on international transfers, "a tenth" only reflects the portion of the market FIFA can see; it excludes domestic deals, contract renewals, and player-side payments captured in FA reports. Targeted caps on wages and renewals could still meaningfully reduce club payouts. Even so, the broader lesson holds: capping the percentage rate will not stop agent fees from climbing as long as underlying transfer spending continues to inflate.

FIFA is heading back to a cap, now with the Court's blessing in principle. By the end of 2027, either the negotiated cap pushes agents' share of international spending below the 8.3% floor of the past seven years, or it will have regulated the rate and left the bill to keep growing with the market.
