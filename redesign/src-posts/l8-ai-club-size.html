Football's need for AI holds below the top. The money does not. (Part 2)

The second part of our study of AI in football. Part 1 mapped where AI is bought across a club's value chain and found the larger gap is the link between activities. This part splits the picture by the size of the club: who needs AI, who can pay for it, and who buys for those that cannot. Parts 3 and 4 follow.

In August 2026 Bolton Wanderers, three months after promotion to the Championship, published a club-wide AI strategy. It was built with Arkero, a Seattle start-up, around what the two called a "club intelligence layer": one set of data, systems and AI agents running across the club, rather than one tool for each department. Bolton is not a rich club by the standards of the Premier League. It is attempting what Part 1 of this study found most clubs lack: a way to join their activities up.

Part 1 mapped where AI is bought across a club's activities, for football as a whole. This part asks whether that picture changes with the size of the club: who needs AI, who can pay for it, and what holds back the clubs that do not buy it.

Four kinds of club

The study sorts clubs into four tiers by annual revenue, and treats leagues and multi-club groups as separate buyers that can purchase on clubs' behalf.

  • Elite, above €300m. Seventeen clubs, according to Deloitte's Football Money League for 2024/25: eight from the Premier League, three each from LaLiga and Serie A, two from the Bundesliga and Paris Saint-Germain. The line is clean: the 17th club, Newcastle, earned €398m; the 18th, Stuttgart, €296m.
  • Upper-mid, €100m to €300m. The rest of the Premier League, where every club now passes €100m, and the larger clubs in Germany, Italy, Spain, France, Portugal and the Netherlands.
  • Mid, €20m to €100m. The Championship is the clearest example: its 24 clubs earned £942m in 2024/25, about £39m each, according to Deloitte.
  • Small, below €20m. League One and League Two, where average revenue was £9.1m and £6.6m in 2023/24, and most of Europe's 700-plus top-division clubs outside the big leagues.

Below the elite, most of that money is already committed to players. A club in that position does not lack reasons to use AI. It lacks room to spend on anything else.

Every club needs AI, for different reasons

Part 1 built need from three measures: the value at stake, urgency, and signals of buying such as hires, tenders and deals. Rated tier by tier, they show that need does not simply follow money (Figure 2).

  • Value at stake is highest where the sums are largest, at the top, but stays high in the middle. Promotion from the Championship is worth at least £205m over three seasons, according to Deloitte, so a better signing can be worth more to a Championship club than to many Premier League ones.
  • Urgency rises as clubs get smaller. Spending rules bite at the top, but revenue cushions them. Lower down there is no cushion: only two of the 24 Championship clubs made a pre-tax profit in 2024/25, and League One clubs lost £7.2m each on average.
  • Buying signals are strongest at the top, where clubs hire data and AI leads, and present but thinner below, where clubs buy single tools.

Put together, need is high for every tier except the smallest clubs, where it is medium mainly because the sums are small. What falls steadily is the ability to pay. The question is whether what suppliers sell meets that need at each size.

Where supply falls short

Using Part 1's method, applied to each tier, supply is assessed on how many suppliers sell to that size of club, how proven their products are and whether prices suit its budget. Gaps are coded by size: a big gap where need sits a full level or more above supply, a mid gap at half a level, a light gap at a quarter. No cell reaches a pocket.

The back office, Part 1's one gap, turns out to be mostly a smaller club's gap. It is a mid gap for the elite and upper-mid, and a big gap for mid-sized and small clubs. A smaller club has fewer people for the office's work, so AI that does it is worth more to it. Cambridge United, the League Two club Part 1 cited for drafting contracts with AI, put it simply: "we don't have an in-house counsel." Genie AI, its supplier, estimates that clubs in the Championship and below spend £50,000 to £350,000 a year on outside lawyers, a vendor's figure but a large one for a club with £7m of revenue.

Elsewhere the gaps are minor. For elite and upper-mid clubs, supply sits level with or ahead of need in most activities; the exception is fans and matchday, where the largest audiences create the most demand and suppliers have not yet caught up. Mid-sized and small clubs are level on recruitment and development.

So the gap inside the activities widens as clubs get smaller, and it is widest in the work smaller clubs have the fewest people to do. That raises the question the rest of this part answers: why does the gap stay open?

Why the gap stays open

Three explanations are possible: the tools cost too much, clubs lack the people to use them, or clubs lack anyone to connect them. The evidence points away from the first and towards the other two.

Is cost the bottleneck?

Mostly not. Football data suppliers rarely publish prices, but the public numbers are small against club budgets:

  • Catapult, the performance-tracking company, disclosed that its average contract with a professional team is worth a little over $30,000 a year, across sports.
  • General AI assistants cost $20 to $30 per user a month at list price: Claude Team at $20 to $25, Microsoft 365 Copilot at $30.

A Catapult-sized contract is under 0.1% of an average Championship club's revenue and about 0.3% of a League Two club's. Cost binds only at the bottom, and only for a full professional set of tools plus the staff to run them.

The people who use AI

What smaller clubs describe instead is a shortage of people. Wrexham, in the Championship, runs its analysis with three analysts. "You've only got so many hours everybody can spend in a day," one told Training Ground Guru in August 2026. Reading's head of AI, Stuart Fenton, warned that AI is not "a light switch". Across all sports, a 2025 survey of 300 executives by SportBusiness and the AI company G42 found 35% naming resistance to change as the biggest barrier. These accounts come mostly from clubs that have already adopted AI, so the barriers are better described than measured. The clubs that have moved show the same pattern:

  • The elite hire the people. Manchester United appointed a director of data in April 2025 to build "a world-class data and AI function".
  • Owner-led clubs bring the skills with them. Brighton's Tony Bloom and Brentford's Matthew Benham both made their money in betting analytics, and both clubs built recruitment around data; Brighton let most of its full-time scouts go in November 2024, according to The Telegraph. Both are upper-mid clubs, not elite.
  • Smaller clubs buy one tool and name one owner. Gillingham bought Analytics FC's TransferLab for recruitment; Reading runs a one-year pilot; Cambridge uses AI for contracts.

The people who connect it

The harder shortage is of people who join a club's data and systems together so that AI can work across them: the connection Part 1 found missing. Clubs are trying three routes:

  • Buy it. Tottenham, Part 1's example, replaced more than 30 systems with one Salesforce platform over two years.
  • Build it. Manchester United's data function and the elite's AI hires are building connected tools inside the club.
  • Rebuild with a partner. Bolton's "club intelligence layer" puts one data foundation under the whole club, built with a start-up rather than a global software company. Arkero raised $6m in January 2026 and named Bolton as its only client in the English Football League.

Each route asks for something different. Buying needs a budget and a supplier willing to serve a club of that size; the platform deals found by this study are all with elite clubs. Building needs data staff, who remain scarce: data and analytics roles were a record 3.2% of hiring by UK clubs in 2026, according to Indeed, from 1.5% in 2018. Rebuilding needs a partner and an owner willing to change how the whole club works. Below the elite, the first route is the least available and the second the hardest to staff, which is why the third is the one to watch.

The gap between football's richest clubs and the rest is not a gap in tools. It is a gap in the people who use AI and the people who connect it, and smaller clubs cannot close it one licence at a time. If they cannot hire those people, someone else has to supply them.

Who buys for the rest

A club that cannot hire its own people can still be supplied by someone who buys once for many. Two kinds of buyer can do that, and so far neither does much in England.

  • Leagues. Only two leagues are known to have put AI-labelled analysis tools into every club: the Saudi Pro League, as Part 1 noted, and Liga MX, which equipped every team with Genius Sports' Performance Studio in May 2026. Others buy data and video platforms for all clubs without calling them AI: the Eredivisie and its second tier with SciSports, LaLiga with its Sportian Performance platform for all 42 clubs, and the USL Championship with Kitman Labs. In England, league deals reach fans, broadcasters and academies. The Premier League's Microsoft deal modernises the league's own operations; Stats Perform's AI insights through Football DataCo are aimed at broadcasters; Kitman Labs serves academies. None gives a first team an AI tool.
  • Multi-club groups. About 147 groups controlled nearly 400 clubs by early 2025, according to the law firm Clifford Chance, and a group can in principle buy once and share. The public evidence is thin. 777 Partners ran a central analytics department across its clubs before its collapse, and Mercury13, a women's group, signed Catapult across all its clubs in March 2026. No major group has announced a group-wide AI platform.

So the answer to who buys for the rest depends on where a club plays. In Saudi Arabia, Mexico and the Netherlands the league does, at least for the data layer. In England, below the Premier League, mostly nobody does. That is the question Part 3 takes up: how the buyer changes from one region to the next.

Conclusion: size decides who can join things up

Every tier of football needs AI; what changes with size is why, and who can act on it. Supply serves the top of the game well. The gap opens as clubs get smaller and is widest in the back office, the work smaller clubs have the fewest people to do. Cost is rarely what keeps it open. People are: those who use AI, and above all those who connect it across a club. The richest clubs can hire them, owner-led clubs bring them, and everyone else needs someone to supply them, which in most of Europe no one yet does.

How clubs join their AI up will decide who benefits from it. By the end of the 2027-28 season, the evidence will show which route works below the elite: buying the connection from a platform, as Tottenham did; building it with hired people; or rebuilding the club's data with a partner, as Bolton is trying. If none takes hold, the connection Part 1 found missing will stay a privilege of the richest clubs.
What to watch
  • Bolton and Arkero:whether the club intelligence layer reaches recruitment, commercial and finance in its first Championship season, the clearest test of the rebuild route.
  • Reading and Score:whether the one-year pilot, which reached its anniversary in November 2026, is renewed, widened beyond transfers or ended.
  • A league buying for first teams in Europe:any European top flight following Saudi Arabia and Liga MX in giving every club AI analysis tools, not only data.
  • A group-wide AI platform:the first major multi-club group to announce one system across its clubs.
  • The squad cost ratio:the Premier League's first assessment under the new rule, and whether spending limits push clubs to buy recruitment and back-office AI.
